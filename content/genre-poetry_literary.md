## What this is

This is the collection's **belletristic layer** — literature for pleasure, the closest
this archive comes to storytelling for its own sake. The catalogue splits it as **Folk
Tale (63%)** and **Secular Literary Work (37%)**, and the titles bear both out.

On one side, the **folk romances and tales**: *Khun Sam Lo*, the great northern
love-tragedy; *Cao saeng moeng*; *Suwattang khanthang*; the *nithan* of the Himavanta
forest (*Tamnan nitan pa mai himapan*) with its mythical beasts. On the other, the
**sung-verse forms** that are the native vehicle of Lanna literature — the *khao* and the
*kham khap* (*Kam khap*, *Kam khap ka long*, *Khao doi …*), poetry meant to be performed
aloud, not silently read. The corpus is also porous in a way the learned genres are not:
a *khao* retelling of Vessantara (*Kam len khao wetsantala*) turns scripture into
entertainment, and a copy of **Phra Aphai Mani** shows Central Thai literature travelling
north into Lanna hands.

For the wider project this is the **living vernacular voice** — the register where the
tradition entertained itself, where oral performance met the page, and the one that
connects most directly to still-living Lanna storytelling and its documented folklore.

## The shape of the collection

Two features set this genre apart from the learned corpora. First, it is the **latest-
dating genre in the collection**: no witness is older than 1836, and the bulk is 20th
century — vernacular literature was written down only recently, long after the scripture
and the grammars. Second, its support and geography break the canonical mould — **paper
runs to 21%** (against the 3–9% of the scholastic genres), and it is **not** a Wat Sung
Men tradition at all: Phayao and Nan lead, Phrae trails, and Tai Lue temples like Wat Nong
Bua figure. The folk literature lived *away* from the great scholastic library.

## Notes

- **The most exposed genre, and the most urgent.** Because these forms are oral and
  performed, and were committed to writing only from the 19th century, this is the corpus
  most under-represented on the shelf — its life is in performance, not the page.
  Because the manuscript record is thin here, the *khao* and *kham khap* material deserves early
  attention despite its small catalogue footprint — much of it lives in the oral and
  contemporary record rather than on palm-leaf.
- **Porous boundaries — it absorbs from everywhere.** Central Thai imports (*Phra Aphai
  Mani*), jātaka retold as entertainment (the *khao* Vessantara), and folk tales that
  shade into [chronicle](/a?s=genre:tamnan_chronicle) all sit here. Expect overlap with
  [jātaka](/a?s=genre:jataka) wherever a birth-story is told for pleasure rather than merit.
- **Border diversity.** A real Tai Lue and Shan presence, higher than the learned genres',
  marks where secular literature crossed ethnic and linguistic lines — folk story travels
  more freely than canon.
- **One label, two objects.** Oral *folk tale* and written *secular literary work* are
  distinct traditions collapsed together; separating them is the obvious next pass.
