## What this is

This is the **wisdom-and-conduct** literature: how to live well, told as maxim,
admonition and proverb. The catalogue files all 175 witnesses cleanly as *Didactics*,
and the titles fall into two clear registers.

On the learned side sit the Pali **nīti** classics — above all the **Lokanīti**, which
appears here several times over: the great anthology of moral maxims that travelled from
Indian *nīti* through Burma into the north, the standard gnomic reader of the region.
Around it cluster the **ovāda** literature (*owāda*, admonition — *Owata tipani*,
*Owatanusasini katha*) and the **subhāsit** (*subhāsita*, "well-spoken" sayings — the
proverb collections).

On the vernacular side sits something warmer and more domestic: the folk-didactic of
elders instructing the young. *Pu thao son lan* — "grandfather teaches grandchild" —
*Manglai son luk* ("Mangrai teaches his children"), *kham son* teaching-verses, and the
sayings of **Phaya In** (Indra) as folk wisdom-figure. Alongside them run texts on
householder conduct (*gihi-cariyā*, *Kihicaritta*) — worldly ethics for the layperson,
not the monk.

For the wider collection this is the **ethical layer**: adjacent to the canon but not
soteriological, adjacent to the *wichaa* but not efficacious. It is where the tradition
records its values rather than its doctrine or its power — the voice of the teacher and
the elder rather than the scribe of scripture.

## The shape of the collection

The physical profile is ordinary for a learned genre — **88% palm-leaf**, **94% Tham
Lanna**, overwhelmingly Pali-and-Lan Na — and it skews **late**, the 20th century
(46 dated witnesses) edging out the 19th, consistent with a living folk-wisdom still being
copied within living memory.

But its *geography* is extraordinary, and is the real finding here — see the first note.

## Notes

- **The flattest geography in the collection.** No library dominates this genre: Phrae,
  Chiang Rai, Phayao and Lampang each hold about a seventh, Chiang Rai stands unusually
  high, and Wat Sung Men — which supplies 40–43% of the canonical and grammar corpora —
  holds barely 9% here. That evenness is not an accident of survival: proverbs and elders'
  teachings were **popular and domestic**, not a monastery monopoly, so they survive
  scattered across every muang rather than concentrated in one great library. This is one
  of the few genres whose provenance counts are a genuinely even regional sample.
- **One label, two registers.** The Pali *nīti* / *subhāsit* classics and the vernacular
  *pu thao son lan* folk-didactic are quite different objects — learned gnomic anthology
  vs. domestic teaching-verse — collapsed under one heading. Splitting them is the obvious
  next pass, and would separate a translated Indian tradition from a native Tai one.
- **Boundary blur with law and astrology.** Several volumes bind didactics to *kotmai
  boran* (old customary law) or to *Holasat* (astrology) — the household-manual pattern
  again, where conduct, law and the calendar share a single book. Check the raw metadata
  and cross-reference [customary law](/a?s=genre:law_customary) when a title names both.
- **Skews late.** The dated witnesses run from the 18th to the 20th century, weighted
  toward the most recent — folk wisdom kept being copied within living memory, the mark of
  a genre that stayed in domestic use rather than freezing into a fixed canon.
