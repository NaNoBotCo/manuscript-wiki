## What this is

This is the **wisdom-and-conduct** literature: 175 witnesses on how to live, filed by
the catalogue under one word, *Didactics*. It is not doctrine and it is not law. It is
the register in which the tradition tells you what people are actually like — and it is
much colder about that than the shelves on either side of it.

The genre's own titles make the temperature plain. **ลักขณะอันถ่อยอันร้ายอันดีทังมวน** —
"the marks of the base, the vile and the good, all of them" — sits at Wat Ban Ueam in
Lampang, with a second untitled copy at [Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/).
**ชายสามโบด ยิงสามผัว**, "the man of three ordinations, the woman of three husbands," is
21 pages with no temple recorded. **สุภาสิตตายิงแปดจำพวกดีสี่บ่อดีสี่** — "the *subhāsit*
of the eight kinds of women, four good and four not good" — is two leaves at
[Wat Pa Sak Noi](/place/wat-pa-sak-noi/), Chiang Mai, 1946. **หมากน้ำเต้าโจมหมากหีนฟู**,
"the gourd assaults the pumice-stone," is six leaves at the same temple, 1949. These are
not homilies. They are a sorting apparatus for human beings.

## Three registers under one word

Read the 175 titles and they fall into three quite different literatures.

**The learned gnomic anthology.** Twenty-one witnesses carry **สุภาสิด / สุภาษิต**
(*subhāsita*, "well-spoken"), twenty-one carry **โอวาท / โอวาด** (*ovāda*, admonition),
and above them sits the **nīti** tradition — for which see [Lokanīti](/a?s=entity:lokaniti),
whose evidence here turns out to be stranger than the label suggests (below).

**The elders' verse.** Eight witnesses are **ปู่เถ้าสอนหลาน** — "grandfather teaches
grandchild" — including copies at Wat Khuean Mueang Pa Kha Tai in Chiang Rai (1958), a
*wohan* gloss at [Wat Lai Hin Luang](/place/wat-lai-hin-luang/) in Lampang, an untitled
copy at [Wat Phra That Lampang Luang](/place/wat-phra-that-lampang-luang/), and one of
1903 with no temple recorded, plus a
**พ่อเถ้าสอรหลาน** ("father-elder teaches grandchild") at Wat Phra That Hariphunchai in
Lamphun. And then the joke that proves the form was alive: one undated bundle is titled
**ปู่สอนหลาน หลานสอนปู่** — *grandfather teaches grandchild, grandchild teaches
grandfather*. The reply-poem exists. Somebody wrote the young person's answer down and a
monastery kept it.

**The instruction of the powerful.** Twelve witnesses turn on **พระยาอิน** — Indra as
folk sage — *Tamnan Phanya In*, *Owat kham son haeng Phanya In*, and
**เทสนาพระยาอินลงมาใข้ชาต**, "the sermon of Phanya In coming down to take birth"
([Wat Huai Khrai Luang](/place/wat-huai-khrai-luang/), Chiang Rai). Alongside them,
**สิรินันทพรามสั่งสอรบ้านเมือง** — "the brahmin Sirinanda instructs the realm" — 14 pages
at [Wat Si Sutthawat](/place/wat-si-sutthawat/), Chiang Rai, dated **1853**; and, at
[Wat Pang Mu](/place/wat-pang-mu/) in Mae Hong Son, a 53-page Shan bundle catalogued as
**ข้อปฏิบัติของพระยาสิบประการ**, "the ten practices of a king." *Tradition holds —* that
last is the *dasarājadhamma*, the ten royal virtues, the standard Southeast Asian
statement of what a ruler owes. It is held here in Shan, on mulberry paper, in a border
province, and it is untitled: the cataloguer had to name it from its contents.

## Where the Lokanīti actually is

The obvious flagship of this genre is the **Lokanīti**, the great anthology of maxims
that travelled from Indian *nīti* through Burma into the north. Search for it by name and
the result is a surprise: **two witnesses**, both **Shan**, both from Mae Hong Son —
**ลอกะนิติ** at Wat Luang (1880, 145 pages) and at Ban Pang Mu (1889, 70 pages). In Tham
Lanna, under that spelling, it is not here at all.

What *is* here, in quantity, is a Pali title-family the north spells
**โลกไนย / โลกนัย / โลกนัยยะ** — *lokanai*, *lokanainya* — thirteen witnesses, and the
catalogue has scattered them across four genres: **seven** under
[jātaka](/a?s=genre:jataka), four under
[Buddhist canonical](/a?s=genre:buddhist_canonical), one under
[customary law](/a?s=genre:law_customary), and only **one** under Didactics. The oldest
of them, **บาลีโลกนัยย** at [Wat Lai Hin Luang](/place/wat-lai-hin-luang/) in Lampang, is
dated **1726** — older than any witness this genre owns under its own label. Wat Ban Luk
in Lampang holds *wohan* glosses of 1743 and 1750; [Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/)
copied it in numbered bundles (*mat ton*, *mat song*, *mat si*) in 1836, several of them
in **Tham Lao** script; Wat Phra Bat Ming Mueang in Phrae has one of 1806, Wat Ban Ueam
in Lampang one of 1814, Wat Suan Tan in Nan one of 1836, Wat Phra That Chang Kham
Worawihan in Nan one of 1860. Only **โลกไนยคำสอร**, at Wat Nantharam in Chiang Mai, wears
the word *kham son* ("teaching") that would have kept it on this shelf.

*Inference —* whether the *lokanai* family is the Lokanīti proper, a related Pali
didactic-narrative work, or both under one spelling, is a question this catalogue cannot
settle from titles alone — and that is precisely the point. **The genre's central text is
mostly filed outside the genre**, because northern scribes gave it a Pali title and later
cataloguers read the Pali title as scripture. Any reader trusting the 175 to be the moral
literature of the north is undercounting it, and the undercount is systematic, not random.

A second family behaves the same way. **โลกหานี / โลกกะหาณี** — twelve witnesses — splits
seven to this genre, four to [chronicle](/a?s=genre:tamnan_chronicle) and one to the canon,
with one copy at Wat Phra That Hariphunchai fusing it to Indra outright as
**ตำนานพระยาอินทาโลกคะหิณี**.

## The maxims themselves

The palm-leaf catalogue records titles, not text, so the sayings inside these 175 bundles
are — for now — unread. But the collection's contributed printed treatises do carry
running text, and they quote the proverb tradition constantly, which gives a fair sample
of the register.

From [the Mind-Current Key to Saiyasat](/m/mind-current-key-saiyasat-6984/) (p.21), on
choosing a teacher, and about as unsentimental as advice gets: *seeing yellow, do not yet
believe it is a good rishi — an old tiger who has attained jhāna becomes a yogi, and once
it gets the chance, it seizes its disciples and eats them.* The same page runs the blind
men and the elephant, and does it as a *teaching* failure rather than a parable of
relativism: the blind teacher grasps the tail, the blind disciples grasp the tail, and
they all sit down to meditate on the certainty that an elephant is shaped like a broom.

From [the Akhom Magic of Phra Narai](/m/akhom-magic-king-narai-era-6970/) (p.46),
introducing a love-mantra with a shrug: *they say love is as thin as a sheet of leather,
and hatred as thin as a mat* — followed by the flat observation that love takes months and
years to root and anger takes the blink of an eye, and that a physician can treat a
sickness but nobody can treat this.

From [the Ancient Traditional Medicine manual](/m/ancient-medicine-manual-6990/) (p.103),
mid-way through a list of fevers, a scribe pauses to remind the reader that *to know a
thing is not the same as carrying it on one's shoulders* — knowledge is no burden. Learn it
anyway.

And [the Compendium of Ancient Katha](/m/compendium-katha-from-old-manuals-6966/) (p.59)
preserves an entire piece of *sawatdi-raksa* — protective conduct — in verse, addressed
upward: *May Your Majesty keep this well in mind… intended for persons of royal and noble
lineage, that they may be clear, at ease, blessed, and free of harm.* Then the copyist
breaks frame and explains himself: the ancient verses were composed in *chan* metre, but
of that generation who now remains to explain them? So he has retold and reworked it, set
it down again as verse, in the hope that it can be learned by heart — offered in repayment
of a debt of gratitude — and if anything has been altered or mistaken from the ancient
telling, he begs pardon for the fault. *Inference —* that apology is the didactic genre's
self-portrait. The material is transmitted by people who know they are changing it, to
audiences who need it memorable more than they need it exact.

The corpus also holds the cold-eyed extreme of this register, in a form worth flagging
rather than admiring. The 20th-century printed
[physiognomy manual for reading women](/m/reading-women-physiognomy-ngo-heng-head-toe-6973/)
(p.172) works through female character type by type: the woman who pursues her own
advantage will never keep company with anyone who can see through her, and any man who
marries her *must first resign himself to becoming a tame water-buffalo, letting his wife
lead him by the nose whenever she pleases — only then can the couple stay together.* It is
a printed Central Thai manual, not a Lanna palm-leaf, and it is not evidence for what the
175 witnesses say. But it is the same genre-instinct running on, and it explains why the
northern titles about "eight kinds of women, four good and four not" should be expected to
read as a taxonomy rather than a compliment.

## Where the stories went

The teaching shelf and the story shelf trade in both directions.

Vidhura the wise minister — the Bodhisatta as royal counsellor, hero of one of the Ten
Births — appears sixteen times in [jātaka](/a?s=genre:jataka), where he is a tale. He
appears three more times here, where he is not: **วิธุรชัยสอรลูก** (Vidhura teaches his
children), **เจ้าวิธูรสอรหลาน** (Lord Vidhura teaches his grandchildren, on khoi paper),
and **คลองวิธูรใช้สอนโลก** — "the way Vidhura used to teach the world" — six pages at
[Wat Pratu Pa](/place/wat-pratu-pa/) in Lamphun, dated **1964**. The narrative has been
boiled off and only the counsel kept. **มหาสาริบุดสอรแม่**, "Sāriputta teaches his mother,"
two leaves at [Wat San Rim Ping](/place/wat-san-rim-ping/), does the same to a canonical
episode. And **คุรุธัมมชาดก** at Wat Phaya Phu in Nan (1935) keeps the word *chadok* in its
title while sitting on this shelf, its siblings *Kulutham* and *Kulutham kam son* having
dropped it.

Traffic runs the other way too. Three copies of **ตำนานดาวควัน**, the smoke-star tale, are
filed here as moral instruction — at Wat Sung Men, Wat Si Khom Kham and Wat Phra That Si
Chom Thong (1963) — while a fourth sits under [poetry](/a?s=genre:poetry_literary) as a
folk tale. And **โวหารนิทานสอรผู้ยิงผู้ชาย**, "the tale-discourse instructing women and
men" (Wat Don Can, Chiang Rai, 1951), and
**นิทานโวหารโอวาท** at Wat San Rim Ping, both keep *nithan* — story — in the title while
claiming the didactic label. *Inference —* in practice the north did not distinguish
sharply between a story and a lesson; it distinguished between a story told **for** the
lesson and a story told for itself, and the catalogue's genre boundary falls somewhere
inside that distinction rather than cleanly on either side.

## The founder on the teaching shelf

King [Mangrai](/a?s=entity:mangrai)'s name runs through the northern shelves mostly as
law — **seventeen** witnesses of *Mangraisat*, *Kotmai Phanya Mangrai*, *Khong Mangrai*
and the like under [customary law](/a?s=genre:law_customary), on which see
[Mangraisat](/a?s=entity:mangraisat) and [Thammasat](/a?s=entity:thammasat). But the
founder also turns up teaching, and where he does is instructive. On this shelf, at
Wat Phra That Chang Kham Worawihan in Nan, a 55-page mulberry-paper bundle is catalogued
as **มังรายสอนลูก, คำสอนเถรจันท์ ฯลฯ** — "Mangrai teaches his children, the teaching of
Thera Chan, etc." Just off it, at
[Wat Si Khom Kham](/place/wat-si-khom-kham-phayao/) in Phayao, a bundle of 1863 pairing
**โอวาทคำสอนพระยามังราย** with **เฒ่าสอนหลาน** — the elder-and-grandchild verse — has
been filed under the [canon](/a?s=genre:buddhist_canonical); and **พระมังรายสอนโลก**,
"Mangrai teaches the world," sits at Wat Luang Ratchasanthan in Phayao under the law
label. *Inference —* the same royal name
underwrites the code and the copybook. In the north, "what the law is" and "what a
grandfather says" are transmitted by the same authority, in the same bundles, and
frequently in the same hand.

## The shape of the collection

The physical profile is ordinary for a learned genre — **154 of 175 on palm-leaf (88%)**,
**164 in Tham Lanna (94%)**, with a small Shan minority of nine, all of it in Mae Hong
Son, and one Tham Lao. Only sixteen witnesses are on mulberry paper and one on khoi.

Dating: **81 dated witnesses, running 1742 to 1974** — four in the 18th century, 31 in the
19th, **46 in the 20th**. The oldest is **โอวาทานุสาสินีคาถา**, Wat Sung Men, **1742**;
then **เทวมุลคันถี** at Wat Phra Luang in Phrae (1771), **หนังสือโอวาทวิไนยคำสอน** at
[Wat Duang Di](/place/wat-duang-di/) in Chiang Mai (1789), and **โวหารเจตนาเภทา** at Wat
Ban Luk in Lampang (1799). The 20th-century weighting is not decay; it is a genre still in
domestic use when the copying stopped.

But the **geography** is the real finding, and it is the flattest in the whole collection —
see the first note.

## Notes

- **The flattest geography in the collection.** No library dominates. Phrae (25),
  Chiang Rai (25), Phayao (24) and Lampang (23) each hold about a seventh of the genre;
  Chiang Mai (18), Lamphun (17), Nan (10) and Mae Hong Son (9) fill in behind. Chiang Rai
  stands unusually high, and [Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/) — which supplies 40–43%
  of the canonical and grammar corpora and a fifth of the [jātaka](/a?s=genre:jataka) —
  holds **16 witnesses here, 9%**. The largest single holder is
  [Wat Si Khom Kham](/place/wat-si-khom-kham-phayao/) in Phayao, at 15. That evenness is
  not an accident of survival: proverbs and elders' teachings were **popular and domestic**,
  not a monastery monopoly, so they survive scattered across every muang rather than
  concentrated in one great library. This is one of the few genres whose provenance counts
  make a genuinely even regional sample, and it is worth reading on the [atlas](/atlas)
  against the jātaka distribution, which is the opposite case.
- **The label is undercounting the genre.** As above: the *lokanai* family — the plausible
  core of the northern *nīti* tradition, and the oldest material in the neighbourhood at
  1726 — is filed 12 witnesses outside this genre and 3 inside it. A reader working from
  [browse](/browse?genre=didactic_moral) will not see it. Any recount of this genre should
  begin by pulling the Pali-titled *nīti* material back in from
  [jātaka](/a?s=genre:jataka) and the [canon](/a?s=genre:buddhist_canonical).
- **One label, three registers.** The Pali *nīti* and *subhāsit* anthologies, the domestic
  *pu thao son lan* teaching-verse, and the royal-conduct literature (*Phanya In*, the ten
  royal practices, *Mangrai teaches his children*) are three distinct objects — a
  translated Indian tradition, a native Tai one, and a statecraft one. Splitting them is
  the obvious next pass.
- **Boundary blur with law and the calendar.** Several bundles bind didactics to *kotmai
  boran* (old customary law) — one at Wat Phra That Chang Kham Worawihan, dated 1907, is
  catalogued as *Kotmai bolan, Tamnan Phanya In* — or to
  [Holasat](/a?s=genre:astrology): two bundles at Wat Pa Mueat in Nan pair
  *pu thao son lan* with astrology outright. This is the household-manual pattern, where
  conduct, law and the calendar share one book, and it is a reason to cross-reference
  [customary law](/a?s=genre:law_customary) whenever a title names both.
- **A short, cheap transcription target.** Unlike the long *nīti* anthologies, the
  vernacular titles here are tiny — the eight-kinds-of-women *subhāsit* is two leaves, the
  *Sāriputta teaches his mother* is two, most *kham son* copies four to eight. Their whole
  content is quotable sayings, which makes them unusually high-yield per page transcribed
  and an ideal feed for the [glossary](/glossary/) and the [knowledge graph](/graph).
