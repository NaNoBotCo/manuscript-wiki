## What this is

**Khun Phaen** (ขุนแผน) is the hero of the Thai epic *Khun Chang Khun Phaen* —
soldier, seducer, and master of the wichaa — and the only figure in this archive
who is at once a literary character, an invoked teacher, a family of na, katha,
and yant, and one of the most-traded amulet classes on the living market. Most
subjects here are a text-type or a being; Khun Phaen is a *person the tradition
kept using after his story ended*. This page tells enough of the story to make
the objects legible, then follows him through the printed treatises the corpus
holds and out onto the market that still trades under his name — where he turns
out to be the single most instructive object in the whole amulet taxonomy: a
pressed tablet in **Buddha-amulet form** made with **prai-kuman material**, a
hybrid whose two halves are both, as it happens, in the epic.

## The story, first

*Tradition holds —* the epic ran for centuries as oral *sepha* recitation,
performers rapping krap sticks in market squares and temple fairs, before the
Bangkok court fixed it in print. It begins with an execution: the boy Phlai
Kaeo's father, a royal officer, botches a buffalo roundup and is put to death
by the king, and the family scatters. The fatherless boy ordains as a novice —
and turns out to be brilliant at exactly the curriculum this archive
catalogues. In the monasteries of Suphanburi and Kanchanaburi he learns
invulnerability, invisibility, love-charms, and the raising of spirits; his
teacher Ajarn Khong of Wat Khae is remembered for lessons like enchanting
tamarind leaves into wasps — the temple still shows visitors the tamarind
tree. Note what the epic has done here: the greatest magician in Thai
literature is monastery-made. His power wears a monk's form from the first
page.

*Tradition holds —* the rest of his life runs on the two engines the amulets
still advertise, เสน่ห์ and คงกระพัน — charm and invulnerability. Still a
novice, he wins Phim Philalai (later renamed Wanthong) at a Songkran festival;
summoned to war on his wedding night, he returns victorious from the north to
find his rich, bald rival Khun Chang has faked his death and married her. The
epic's most famous night-scene is a working demonstration of this page's
subject matter: Khun Phaen enters Khun Chang's compound with charms that put
the whole household into enchanted sleep, walks room by room past the guards,
and carries Wanthong off on horseback into the forest — where they live as
fugitives while the kingdom hunts them. Readers have always noticed the moral
weather of this: the same man is the epic's most devout and its most
dangerous, and the poem never resolves the tension. Neither do the amulets.

*Tradition holds —* the Golden Boy is made in the darkest of these fugitive
years. Bua Khli, a bandit chief's daughter Khun Phaen has married on the run,
is ordered by her father to poison him; warned in time, he cuts his unborn son
from her body, carries the child to the ordination hall of a temple, and
roasts him over a fire through the night, chanting, while the hall's
consecrated boundary holds every rival spirit out — until at dawn the boy's
spirit speaks, and serves his father ever after as scout, sentry, and spy.
This is the origin story the kuman-thong practice cites for itself, and the
scene the amulet trade never forgot: when a modern maker presses prai-kuman
powder into a tablet stamped with Khun Phaen's name, this night is the
warrant.

*Tradition holds —* the epic does not end in his triumph. It ends with the
king demanding that Wanthong choose at last between her two men; she stands
silent — the poem lets her be the only honest person at court — and is
executed for it, her son's royal pardon arriving minutes too late. The hero
outlives everyone he charmed. That, too, is in the amulets: nothing about
Khun Phaen's magic promises a happy ending — it promises *effect*.

Two facts from all this do the work below: his magic is monastery-trained,
Buddhist in form; and the kuman thong is his making. Form and material,
already assigned.

## The market walk — ชมตลาด

*Tradition holds —* the name **ขุนแผนชมตลาด**, "Khun Phaen admires the
market," remembers the hero strolling a market street with every head turning
as he passes — admiring, and admired. The corpus carries the katha itself, in
plain first-person Thai rather than Pali (*vedic-katha-for-chai-chatri*
p.90–91), and it is a strut set to a drumbeat: "Om, may I succeed! …my glory
is as beautiful as the sky, my arms as beautiful as Narayana's, my radiance
the sun's, my power the moon's; not one maiden in the heavenly city can help
but long for my face." Then the boast climbs a ladder of similes, each rung
punctuated *นะ มะ พะ ทะ*: may they be dazed as the swan-king who swallowed
water and lost himself; as the great fish who forgot Mother Ganges; may kings
forget their thrones and beds, the servant girl forget her mistress, the old
woman fall in love, the devout man forget his prayers — and "the young hen
abandon her chicks and come running after me," the [kai](/a?s=entity:kai)
making its cameo in another bird's register. After all that thunder, the
instructions are a cosmetics counter: chant it over face powder, perfumed
hair-oil, lip-wax; it works "both toward noble patrons and toward women." A
war-god's speech, deployed via makeup.

The same name attaches to a drawn na. The Scripture of 108 Magical Na gives
**นะขุนแผนชมตลาด** twice (*scripture-of-108-magical-na* p.13, p.43 — one copy
credited to Wat Samrong), and its inscription formula is its own best gloss:
the na is built in six recited steps — *first, the princess; second, the
prince; third, a woman; fourth, a man; fifth, a female merchant; sixth, a male
merchant* — the market crowd assembled person by person into the glyph before
it is consecrated for "loving-favor from all people." *Inference —* the sutra
answers the question the name poses: what does Khun Phaen see when he admires
the market? Everyone in it, one by one, and the na holds them all.

## The soldier's half

The love-charm is only half the repertoire; the epic's Khun Phaen is a
soldier, and his corpus keeps the soldier's tools — with the epic itself
cited as the case record.

The best story in the set is the **invisibility katha**
(*vedic-katha-for-chai-chatri* p.86), whose instructions read like a
fugitive's field manual, because that is what they are. Sit — leaning against
a tree is fine — with your feet drawn in to close the body's openings, and
recite without letting the words drop: no one will see you. If several
pursuers are closing in, the treatise offers the most audacious sentence in
the corpus: have your companions *sit stacked one on top of another, and ride
on top of the stack* — the whole tower is shielded. Traveling by boat,
consecrate three sprigs of the "leaf that knows how to sleep" and fix them at
bow, midship, and stern; you will glide past the customs post unchallenged.
And then the treatise names its authority — not a monk, but the poem: "in the
story of Khun Chang Khun Phaen, this very katha was used, consecrating a hing
leaf to blind pursuers' eyes all the way to Chiang Mai." The epic is cited
the way a law report cites precedent: it worked for the fugitive in the tale;
it will work for you.

- **นะขุนแผน (นะกำบัง)** — the Concealment Na (*scripture-of-108-magical-na*
  p.51, credited to Luang Pu Chaeng) runs the same logic in drawn form: each
  stroke of the five-step inscription is paired with a hiding — *na* wards,
  *mo* closes, *phut* hides completely, *tha* surrounds the body, *ya* hides
  the head.
- **พระคาถามหาชาตรีขุนแผน** — the Great Warrior katha
  (*vedic-katha-for-chai-chatri* p.81–82): a long syllable-string chanted
  over food, liquor, or sesame oil for invulnerability, with a testing
  protocol included — "try hacking at it with an axe; the blade can do no
  harm at all" — and a plain attribution: "it is a katha of Khun Phaen."
- **หัวใจขุนแผน** — the whole man compressed to four syllables, *สุนะโมโล*
  (*biography-of-lp-thob-and-his-katha* p.104): chant it over the clothes you
  wear, for popularity. The same four syllables sit in the mnemonic
  curriculum tables of two separate treatises (*vedic-katha-for-chai-chatri*
  p.50; *yantra-inscription-formulas-khom-thai* p.20), filed between the
  Yaksha King and the Diamond Armor — Khun Phaen as a standard entry in the
  syllable canon, alongside suttas and gods.

A further na-index page (*scripture-of-108-magical-na* p.96) shows
**นะขุนแผน** as one glyph in a gallery of twenty-five named na — between the
Tiger and the World-Devastator — each a little machine of zigzags, loops, and
spiral coils. Whatever else he is, in these books Khun Phaen is a *repertoire
slot*: a name a na, a katha, a heart-syllable, and a yant can each be filed
under.

## Worn, inked, and held

- **ยันต์สาริกาลิ้นทองขุนแผน** — the Golden-Tongued Mynah of Khun Phaen
  (*yantra-inscription-formulas-khom-thai* p.55): a teardrop yant inscribed on
  a takrut and worn *in the mouth*, consecrated 108 times — sweet speech,
  carried at the point of speech, for a hero whose voice talked its way into
  guarded houses.
- **นะขุนแผนมหาเสน่ห์** — a sak-yant stencil
  (*sak-yant-stencils-for-tracing*): two five-spired
  pagoda-shaped charts of Khom syllables, ready to trace
  onto skin. The na has left the paper for the body.
- **The verse "used with a Phra Khun Phaen"**
  (*biography-of-lp-thob-and-his-katha* p.102) comes wrapped in its own
  initiation drama. To learn it at all, the teacher requires a formal
  homage-rite — five kinds of flowers, five candles, five incense sticks,
  begun on a Thursday. Then chant it over lip-wax or face powder — it has a
  slot where you *say the beloved's name* — and use it "together with a Khun
  Phaen amulet, to excellent effect." Printed right beneath: the teachers
  forbid passing this knowledge on, "trust this and never violate it." This
  is the bridge sentence of the whole page: the printed katha reaches out and
  touches the market object, and the object is expected to be there.
- **The powder and the held image** (*mind-stream-key-to-saiyasat*
  p.110–111) is a story told in the first person. A master dreams an
  instruction: gather the flowers monks offer each other at the rite of
  mutual forgiveness before the Rains Retreat — from 108 temples in a single
  day — with moss from a relic stupa and from a sacred Bodhi tree, and press
  Buddha images from them. He wakes joyful, sends word to disciples across
  every province, and among the relics that come back is one he names with a
  straight face: "the special powder of Ajarn Khong, *teacher of Khun
  Phaen*," dug from beneath a stupa in Si Prachan district, Suphanburi — the
  epic's home ground yielding physical provenance for a literary teacher,
  complete with its own history attached. The method of use that follows:
  hold the image in the hand, breathe *phut* in and *tho* out until the mind
  settles, then recite "remembering Ajarn Khong and Ajarn Khun Phaen" until
  the deva comes down to possess the medium. *Inference —* the treatises
  handle Khun Phaen precisely as they handle historical masters: his teacher
  leaves relics, his name is called in invocation, his lineage warrants
  powder. Whether he "existed" is not a question the shelf asks.

## A Buddha's form, a kuman's material

On the living market the hybrid is measurable. Of the 13,020 amulet listings
in the [market layer](/w/products)'s Lazada snapshot, **237 name ขุนแผน in the
title**. The classic form is a pressed tablet showing a seated Buddha — form
*phra khrueang*, indistinguishable at arm's length from any votive amulet —
but the material tells the other half: 126 of the 237 mention *phrai* in some
form, 101 say **ผงพราย** (prai powder) outright, 91 say **พรายกุมาร**, and 67
claim *kru* (excavated-cache) provenance. The effect vocabulary is the
chom-talat katha's own: เมตตา (71), เสน่ห์ (63), มหานิยม (38), ค้าขาย (14) —
and three listings sell a รุ่น named **ชมตลาด**, the manuscript katha's title
running as a current product name. Prices sit low and lively: the middle half
of the market runs ฿84–257, median ฿173, and the 95 listings that report
sales have moved some 8,700 units between them. This is a folk hero with
distribution.

The taxonomy note in the archive's own class vocabulary reads the seam
carefully: of 389 listings naming กุมาร at all, 106 are Khun Phaen — and those
106 must *not* be read as free-standing kuman, dolls to raise and feed. The
Khun Phaen is a true hybrid: **Buddha-amulet form, prai-kuman material** — the
upkeep of an amulet, the provenance-sensitivity of prai. A scanner that tags
it "kuman" because the word is present is honest about the word and wrong
about the object. *Inference —* the hybrid is the epic compressed to a
pressed tablet: the man trained in the monastery (the Buddha's form, outside)
who roasted his son into a servant-spirit (the kuman's material, within). The
amulet is his biography in cross-section.

The market keeps drifting the form toward the story, too: sixteen listings
are **ขุนแผนอุ้มนาง** — Khun Phaen *holding the maiden* — the tablet giving
up the Buddha-image altogether and pressing the epic scene itself, the
horseback abduction from Khun Chang's house rendered at pendant scale.
And the reference treatise's entry for the class (*the bilingual charm
treatise*, entry A·04) files him as "charm of the irresistible warrior," a
moulded tablet from the Ayutthaya epic "often carrying a Plai Kuman guardian
spirit," and names the line collectors name first: **ขุนแผนพลายกุมาร of
Luang Pho Kuay, Wat Khositaram** (Chainat).

## What the catalogue holds

Search the palm-leaf catalogue and it goes silent: **ขุนแผน scores zero across
all 6,990 titles** — as does ขุนช้าง. Every witness on this page is a
contributed printed treatise from a Central Thai press. The silence has two
readings, and both are probably true. First, the lesson the
[paṭiloma page](/a?s=entity:patiloma) already taught: this register lives at
*section level*, inside untitled ยันต์-and-คาถา compendia a title search cannot
see — a นะขุนแผน may well be waiting, unindexed, in a Nan or Lampang bundle.
Second, the geography: the epic is Ayutthaya's and Suphanburi's, and in it
Chiang Mai is the *other side* — the campaign's far city, the enemy capital
the hero rides against. *Inference —* the Lanna shelf kept its own heroes and
let the lowland keep this one; yet the epic itself knew the road north — its
fugitives flee *to* Chiang Mai, hing leaf in hand — and the printed treatises
that carry his katha circulated everywhere. A close read of the northern
compendia, not a title search, is what would settle where Khun Phaen's wichaa
actually traveled.
