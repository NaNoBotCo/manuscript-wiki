## What this is

This is the **infrastructure of literacy**: the grammatical and lexical apparatus by
which the north learned to read Pali and to write its own Tham script. The catalogue
files all 389 witnesses under a single clean label, *Philology*, and the titles resolve
into three tight families.

First, the **saddā / vyākaraṇa** tradition — Pali grammar proper, the Kaccāyana school
inherited from Pagan: *Saddabhedacintā*, the *Saddavutti* and its *ṭīkā*, the treatises
on *sandhi* (euphonic combination — *Pali sūt sondhi*, *Sap santhi*), and the
*Vuttodaya*, the classic manual of Pali prosody, here both bare and as a *nissaya*
(*Nisai wuttotanya*). Second, the **lexicography** — the *sap* (from Sanskrit *śabda*):
Pali-and-vernacular word-lists and glossaries, *Nama sangkaha*, *Nama sap pali palivan*,
the collected vocabularies a student needed at his elbow. Third, and most immediately
useful to a modern reader, the **vernacular primers**: manuals like *Tamra rian akson
Lanna*, "a treatise for learning the Lanna letters" — the schoolbook by which the script
itself was taught.

So this is the **meta-genre**. Every other corpus in the collection — canonical, chronicle,
*wichaa* — stands on this one, because none of it is legible without the grammar and the
glossary that made Pali construable in the north. It matters to the esoteric material in
a specific way: the *saddā* sciences are also what let a ritualist parse and compose the
Pali *katha* (spell-formulae) at the heart of [Magic & Ritual](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual) —
grammar is the key that turns a line of Pali into a working spoken formula.

## The shape of the collection

This is the most technically "canonical" genre in the collection by its physical and
linguistic profile. It is **97% palm-leaf** — the highest of any genre, a pure scholastic
support — and it carries the **highest share of monolingual Pali** anywhere in the collection
(38%, against 59% Pali-and-Lan Na): a grammar of Pali is very often written *in* Pali. The
dated witnesses cluster hard in the **19th century** (202 of 267), tailing back to the 16th.

Geographically it repeats the canonical pattern — Phrae 50%, and Wat Sung Men alone
supplying 40% of the genre — which is a fact about one library, not one region (see below).

## Notes

- **A library artifact, sharpened.** **Wat Sung Men** in Phrae holds 154 of these 389
  witnesses — **40% of the genre from one temple**. A great teaching monastery's library
  will inevitably over-represent the teaching corpus; read the province and temple counts
  here as *where the grammars were kept and copied*, not where grammar was uniquely studied.
- **One label, three uses.** "Philology" collapses (a) the *saddā / vyākaraṇa* grammars,
  (b) the *sap* lexicons and glossaries, and (c) the vernacular **script-primers**. The
  primers especially deserve pulling out: they are few, but they are the pedagogical key,
  and directly relevant to anyone — human or OCR — learning to read this collection today.
- **A Tham Lao seam.** Nearly a fifth of the witnesses (74, 19%) are in **Tham Lao** rather
  than Tham Lanna — a markedly higher cross-border share than the canonical genres, and a
  pointer to how the Pali grammatical curriculum travelled across the Lao–Lanna world.
- **Directly useful now.** Because this genre *is* the reading apparatus, its glossaries
  and primers are the natural companions to the image-download and OCR phases — the
  collection's own built-in dictionary.
- **Copying range.** The dated witnesses run from the late 16th to the late 20th century,
  clustering hard in the 19th — the age when the northern teaching monasteries were copying
  their curriculum most intensively.
