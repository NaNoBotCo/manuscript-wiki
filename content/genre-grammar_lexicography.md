## What this is

Every other shelf in this archive rests on this one. The catalogue holds **389
witnesses** here, all 389 raw-labelled *Philology* by the source cataloguers, and
what they are is **the apparatus by which a man who spoke Northern Thai learned to
read a sacred language he did not speak**.

That is the whole story, and it is worth saying plainly before the counts start.
A novice in a Lampang monastery in 1774 could not read Pali. Nobody could — Pali
had no native speakers anywhere in the world and had not had any for something like
two thousand years. Yet the canon was in Pali, the liturgy was in Pali, and the
[katha](/a?s=entity:katha) that made a spell work were in Pali. The gap between the
boy and the book had to be bridged by *technology*, and the technology is what these
389 manuscripts are. For every grammar book on these shelves there are roughly six
[canonical texts](/a?s=genre:buddhist_canonical) and four
[birth-stories](/a?s=genre:jataka) that only open because the grammar book exists.

## The nissaya — reading a language you cannot speak

Thirty-six of the 389 are **นิไส / นิสสัย** — *nissaya*. This is the single most
consequential invention in the corpus, and it is not a book so much as a **format**.

Here is the format. You take the Pali sentence. You cut it into its words, in
order. After each Pali word you write its vernacular equivalent, introduced by
*ว่า* — "means." Then the next word. Then the next. The Pali is never replaced; it
is **interleaved**, so what the reader has in front of him is the sacred text and a
running translation braided into one line, in the same hand, on the same leaf.
Read the Pali aloud and you are chanting scripture. Read the glosses and you are
reading Northern Thai. Read them alternately, which is what a student did, and you
are being taught.

The archive holds one such line, transcribed, and it is worth quoting because it is
so ordinary. On a badly damaged mulberry-paper leaf at Wat Ban Ueam in Lampang
(ms 5076, p.6) — a manuscript that has fallen into the
[Other](/a?s=genre:other) bucket, of all places — almost the entire page has rotted
past reading. One clause survives intact:

**ปาสาโท ว่า ปราสาท** — *pāsādo* means *prasat*, a palace.

Four words. Pali noun, the hinge-word "means," vernacular noun. That is the entire
mechanism, and it is the reason a village monk could work through a canonical text
at all. Multiply that line by the length of a sutta and you have a nissaya;
multiply it by a curriculum and you have this genre.

The nissaya witnesses here show how far the format travelled. Most are glosses on
grammar itself — **นิไสสนธิ** at Wat Ban Pang in Lamphun, dated 1727, twelve pages;
**นิไสการก** at [Wat Lai Hin Luang](/place/wat-lai-hin-luang/), dated 1774;
**นิไสวุตโตทยะ**, a nissaya on the manual of Pali *prosody*, in three separate
copies. Some go up a level: ms 2091 at [Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/), dated
1836, is **นิไสอภิธานสับ**, a nissaya on the great Pali thesaurus, 83 pages of
glossing a dictionary. Ms 3811 at [Wat Duang Di](/place/wat-duang-di/) is
**นิไสสุโพลังการ**, a nissaya on the classical manual of poetic ornament — the
technique reaching past grammar into literary theory.

And it escaped the monastery's language classes entirely. Over in
[customary law](/a?s=genre:law_customary), ms 3940 at Wat Sung Men, dated 1827, is
**นิไสคดีโลก** — *a nissaya of worldly cases*. Somebody construed the law code
phrase by phrase into Northern Thai so it could be taught the way scripture was
taught. *Inference —* the nissaya is not a genre. It is a **verb**. Anything hard
could be nissaya'd, and the north nissaya'd its law.

## The eight doors

Look at what the titles repeat, and the corpus stops looking like a heap and starts
looking like a syllabus.

Sixty-three titles begin with **สับ / ศัพท์** — *sap*, from Sanskrit *śabda*,
"word" — and what follows *sap* is almost always one of the same short list of
technical terms:

- **สนธิ** *sandhi* — how sounds fuse where words meet (32 titles touch it)
- **นาม** *nāma* — nouns and their declension
- **การก** *kāraka* — the case-relations, who does what to whom
- **สมาส** *samāsa* — compound words
- **ตัทธิต** *taddhita* — secondary derivation, making words from words
- **อาขยาต** *ākhyāta* — the verb (36 titles)
- **กิต / กิพพิธาน** *kita / kibbidhāna* — primary derivation from verbal roots
- **อุณณาท** *uṇādi* — the leftover suffixes, the irregulars

*Tradition holds —* those are the divisions of **Kaccāyana**, the oldest and most
widely taught Pali grammar in the Theravada world, inherited by Lanna from Pagan.
A student did not read Kaccāyana cover to cover. He worked one division at a time,
and each division was copied out as its own fascicle, which is exactly why this
genre's titles look the way they do.

The proof is in a single manuscript. Ms 1507, [Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/),
dated 1840, 45 pages, is catalogued as:

**ตัวบาฬีสนธิ, ตัวบาฬีนาม, ตัวบาฬีสมาส, ตัวบาฬีตัทธิตตะ, ตัวบาฬีอาขยาต,
ตัวบาฬีกิตตะ, ตัวบาฬีอุณณาท, ตัวบาฬีการก**

Sandhi, nouns, compounds, secondary derivation, verbs, primary derivation, the
irregular suffixes, and the cases. **All eight, in one bundle.** That is not a
manuscript. That is a course, boxed.

It is not alone. Ms 5262 at Wat Ban Luk, Lampang, dated 1756, runs seven of the
eight. Ms 4915 at [Wat Phra That Lampang Luang](/place/wat-phra-that-lampang-luang/),
dated 1760, gives five of them each labelled **กัปป** — *kappa*, "chapter" — which is
Kaccāyana's own word for its divisions. And ms 6246 at Wat Tha Pong in Chiang Mai,
dated 1896, does something a teacher will recognise instantly: seven fascicles
titled **อุททาหอร-** *uddāharaṇa*, "worked examples" — sandhi examples, kita
examples, kāraka examples, uṇādi examples, samāsa examples, nāma examples, ākhyāta
examples. The exercise book, filed next to the textbook.

*Inference —* this is the strongest evidence in the whole archive that Lanna's
monasteries ran a **standardised curriculum**. Eight named units, taught in
fascicles, with a matching set of exercises, copied in Lampang in 1756 and in Chiang
Mai in 1896 under recognisably the same headings. A hundred and forty years apart,
the same syllabus.

## The word-hoard

The second family is the dictionaries, and the north kept the good ones.

Ms 651 at Wat Na Pang, Nan, is **อภิธานสับ** — the *Abhidhānappadīpikā*, the
standard Pali thesaurus of the Theravada world, arranged by subject rather than
alphabet, so that a reader looking for a word for "elephant" finds all of them at
once. Wat Sung Men holds it in depth: two ṭīkā (sub-commentary) copies, ms 1449 and
ms 1459, both dated 1836, the larger at 91 pages; a further copy as
**บาลีพระอภิธานปทีปนี** (ms 2102); and the nissaya at ms 2091. Four different
apparatus-layers on one dictionary, in one library, in one year of copying.

Alongside it sit **บาฬีรัตนมาลา** *Ratanamālā*, "the garland of jewels" (ms 1321),
**นามสังคหะ** *Nāmasaṅgaha* (ms 53), and **นามสัพปาลีปะริวาร** (ms 52, dated 1590) —
the collected vocabularies a student kept at his elbow. This is not lexicography as
scholarship. It is lexicography as **furniture**.

## The oldest thing on the shelf is chapter two

Two hundred and sixty-seven of the 389 carry a date, and they run from **1574 to
1990** — 5 in the sixteenth century, 1 in the seventeenth, 26 in the eighteenth,
**203 in the nineteenth**, 32 in the twentieth. (An earlier survey of this genre put
the nineteenth-century figure at 202; on re-query it is 203.)

The single oldest dated witness is ms 3804 at [Wat Duang Di](/place/wat-duang-di/) in
Chiang Mai, **สมาส ผูกสอง**, dated **1574**, thirteen pages. Translated: *Compounds,
fascicle two.*

Sit with that. The oldest dated grammar in this archive is not a treatise on the
nature of language or a preface in praise of the Buddha's speech. It is **the second
half of the chapter on compound words**, thirteen pages long, and somebody kept it
for four hundred and fifty years. The next year, 1575, produced ms 5401 at Wat Ban
Luk in Lampang — a nissaya on the *Saddabhedacintā*, 54 pages. Ms 58, dated 1584,
holds the *Saddabhedacintā* itself at Wat Phra Sing Woramahawihan in Chiang Mai.
Only five witnesses in this genre are sixteenth-century at all, and here is the
whole list: the compounds fascicle (1574), the *Saddabhedacintā* twice — once
nissaya'd (1575) and once bare (1584) — the *Yojanā-saddā* (1588), and a Pali
word-list (1590). *Inference —* what survived four hundred years here is not
grammar in the abstract but **the handful of specific manuals the north actually
taught from**, plus the machinery for construing them.

The largest item in the genre is ms 1547 at [Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/),
**โยชนาสัททา** *Yojanā-saddā*, dated **1588**, at 155 pages — a full construing
commentary on the grammar. Wat Sung Men holds three copies of it, one of them
(ms 1505, 1837) at 142 pages. *Inference —* three heavy copies of the same
construing manual is what a working teaching library looks like: you need enough
copies that a class can be taught from them simultaneously.

## And the schoolbooks

Six witnesses are the pedagogical key, and they are the ones a modern reader can
most nearly use:

- ms 173, **ตำราเรืยนอักษรล้านนา**, "a treatise for learning the Lanna letters,"
  from Ban Pang Mu, Mae Hong Son
- ms 4920 and ms 4921 at [Wat Phra That Lampang Luang](/place/wat-phra-that-lampang-luang/) —
  **ตำราเรียนภาษาไทยยวน** and **ตำราเรียนอักษรและภาษาไทยยวน**, primers for the *Tai
  Yuan* script and language, the north's own tongue rather than Pali
- ms 5583 at [Wat Si Khom Kham](/place/wat-si-khom-kham-phayao/), **ตำราเรียนไทยยวน**
- ms 722 at Wat Kam Ko, Mae Hong Son, dated 1892, **ตำราอักษรศาสตร์**
- ms 4362, **ตำราเรียนอักขระคุรุสาสติ**, "learning the letters, by the teacher's
  instruction"

*Inference —* the Tai Yuan primers matter disproportionately. Everything else in this
genre teaches Pali *to* people who already read Tham script. These teach the **script
itself**, which makes them the only witnesses here addressed to someone who cannot yet
read anything — the first rung of the ladder, and the exact document a modern OCR
effort or a modern student needs most.

## One library, and a road east

Two facts about where these things are, both of which cut against a naive reading of
the numbers.

**Phrae holds 193 of the 389 — 49.6% — and [Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/) alone
holds 154, nearly 40% of the entire genre.** But Wat Sung Men holds 1,780 manuscripts
across all genres; grammar is 8.7% of its shelf, against 5.6% of the collection
overall. *Inference —* Sung Men is not disproportionately grammatical. It is
disproportionately *large*, and it happens to be a teaching monastery, so the
teaching corpus came with it. Read these province counts as **where the grammars
were kept and copied**, not where grammar was uniquely studied. After Phrae:
Lampang 59, Chiang Mai 51, Nan 30, Phayao 21 — a real spread, and Wat Mueang Mo (32),
[Wat Lai Hin Luang](/place/wat-lai-hin-luang/) (25) and
[Wat Duang Di](/place/wat-duang-di/) (15) are substantial second libraries.

**Seventy-four witnesses — 19% — are in Tham Lao rather than Tham Lanna**, a far
higher cross-border share than the canonical genres carry, plus eleven recorded as
*Pali and Lao*, two Shan, and one in the Thai script. *Inference —* the grammatical
curriculum was the most *portable* thing in the tradition. A chronicle belongs to
the muang that wrote it; a jātaka belongs to the sermon; but Kaccāyana's eight
divisions are the same in Luang Prabang as in Lampang, and a copy made anywhere
works everywhere. This genre's Tham Lao seam is a map of the Lao–Lanna scholarly
road, and it is best walked on the [Atlas](/atlas).

## The physical profile, and what it means

**376 of 389 are palm leaf — 96.7%, the highest share of any genre in the
collection** (the canon runs 93.3%, the jātakas 87.9%, and the
[astrology](/a?s=genre:astrology) corpus only 22%). Six are mulberry paper, three
are khoi, four unrecorded.

And **146 of 389 — 37.5% — are monolingual Pali**, again the highest anywhere; the
canon is at 26%, and [customary law](/a?s=genre:law_customary) is at **zero**.

*Inference —* those two numbers describe the same thing from two sides. Palm leaf is
the archival support, prepared and incised for permanence; paper is what you use for
something you expect to consume. A genre at 96.7% leaf is a genre nobody treated as
disposable. And a grammar of Pali written *in* Pali is a book for specialists who
are already inside the language — which is exactly the opposite of the law corpus,
where a text that is not in the vernacular does not function. Put the two genres
side by side and you can see the tradition sorting its books by **who has to
understand them**.

## The archive is doing it again

*Inference, and a note about the present.* The *sap* manuscripts were built by
scanning texts and pulling out the words that needed glossing. This archive's own
`vocab_candidates` table currently holds **474 terms** harvested the same way from
the transcribed printed treatises — flagged, counted, and awaiting a gloss, which is
precisely the *nāmasaṅgaha* procedure with different tools. What comes out the far
end is the [glossary](/glossary/) and the [vocabulary](/vocab). The genre is not
only the historical reading apparatus; it is the **template for the one being built
now**.

## Notes

- **The primers first.** Of everything in this genre, the five or six Tai Yuan
  script-primers are the highest-value transcription targets, because they are the
  documents that teach the reading skill the rest of the archive presumes. They are
  short, they are few, and they are directly useful to a human or a machine learning
  to read this collection today. Start with ms 173 and ms 4921.
- **The eight-division bundles are a curriculum reconstruction waiting to happen.**
  Ms 1507 (all eight), ms 5262 (seven), ms 4915 (five, labelled *kappa*), ms 6246
  (seven fascicles of worked examples). Comparing what each bundle includes and omits
  would reconstruct the actual northern teaching sequence — a genuinely new finding,
  obtainable from title-level data plus a modest transcription pass.
- **The nissaya is the crawl priority.** Thirty-six here, one loose in
  [customary law](/a?s=genre:law_customary), and at least one legible gloss-line
  stranded in [Other](/a?s=genre:other). A search for the *ว่า* hinge-construction
  across transcribed pages would find nissaya passages inside manuscripts that carry
  no nissaya in their title, in any genre. That is a whole-corpus detector, and
  nobody has run it.
- **Grammar is the key to the spell-books too.** The *saddā* sciences are what let a
  ritualist parse and compose a Pali [katha](/a?s=entity:katha); the
  [magic and ritual](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual) corpus is full of syllable-strings
  whose grammar is doing real work. A reader who has done sandhi can hear where a
  katha's joins are.
- **One inherited figure corrected.** The nineteenth-century dated count is 203, not
  202. Everything else in the earlier profile of this genre — 97% leaf, 38%
  monolingual Pali, Phrae at 50%, Wat Sung Men at 40%, 74 Tham Lao at 19% — holds up
  exactly on re-query.
