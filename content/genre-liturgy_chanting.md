## What this is

This is the **performed** end of the Buddhist corpus — not scripture to be studied but
words to be said aloud. The catalogue files all 97 witnesses as *Chanting*, and the titles
lay out the northern liturgical repertoire.

At its centre is the **suat mon** (*sut mon*), the chanted suttas and *paritta*: the
much-loved *Mahāsamaya Sutta* (*Sap mahasamainya*), the *namo*/*buddhaguṇa* invocations
(*Tika namo*, *Putthakun luang*), the fifteen-day cycles of recitation. Around it runs the
distinctively northern **thon** — occasional recitations for the crises of life, and
above all for **death**: *thon* for the newly dead (*thon tai*, *thon phi tai*), *thon*
for the house and home (*thon ban, thon huean*), *thon* to dispel the inauspicious
(*sut thon sappakhuet*). And at the edges sit the **su khwan** soul-callings (*su khwan*
for a person, even for an elephant) and the *ariradhana* invitations to preach — the
*Kam alathana tham wetsantala*, the summons to recite the Vessantara at the Thet Mahachat.

## The saiyasat hinge — why this genre matters to the wichaa

This is the genre where canonical Buddhism and efficacious *saiyasat* are **the same
performance seen from two sides**. A *sut thon sappakhuet* — a recitation to drive off the
inauspicious — a rite against *kalakini* (bad-luck), a volume that binds *sut mon* directly
to *holasat* (astrology): these are protective chanting *as efficacious action*. The
companion article on [Magic & Ritual](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual) describes the benedictory,
consecratory end of saiyasat; this genre *is* that end, filed under "Chanting" because the
technology is a spoken formula and the setting is the temple. For this archive's esoteric
priorities, the liturgy is not a backwater — it is where the protective and apotropaic
core lives in its most public, communal form.

## The shape of the collection

Written overwhelmingly in **Tham Lanna** (94%), with a notable **15% monolingual Pali** —
the chants themselves are Pali, so a share of the corpus needs no vernacular at all. Its
support leans more to **paper (29%)** than the scholastic genres do, exactly as one would
expect of a text opened and read during performance rather than shelved. It is spread
evenly across the upper north (Phrae, Lampang and Nan almost level) and reaches back
further than most — a witness dated to 1541, the 16th century.

## Notes

- **Read it beside saiyasat, and check for hybrids.** The *thon sappakhuet*, *kalakini*
  and *sut-mon-with-holasat* volumes straddle the liturgy / [magic](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual)
  boundary; when a chanting title also names a protective or calendrical element, check the
  raw metadata — the object is often a household ritual manual, not pure liturgy.
- **Heavily funerary and occasional.** The *thon* for the dead, the house and misfortune
  are the practical working half of this genre — the liturgy of the life-cycle. Splitting
  the *paritta* / *sut* from the *thon* from the *su khwan* soul-callings is the obvious
  next pass, and would separate three quite different performance settings.
- **Linked to the birth-stories by performance.** The invitation to preach the Vessantara
  ties this genre to [jātaka](/a?s=genre:jataka) at the Thet Mahachat — the same text is a
  birth-story on one shelf and a recitation cue on another.
