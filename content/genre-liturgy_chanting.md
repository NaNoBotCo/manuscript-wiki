## What this is

This is the **performed** end of the Buddhist corpus — not scripture to be
studied but words to be said aloud, in a room, at an hour, by a named number of
monks. The catalogue files all **97 witnesses** as *Chanting*, and the titles
lay out the northern liturgical repertoire almost as a working diary of the
year: the fortnightly *sut mon*, the *thon* recited over a bad death, the
*suat boek* that opens a rite, the invitation that summons a preacher to begin
the [Vessantara](/a?s=entity:vessantara).

The technology is the same as the [katha](/a?s=entity:katha)'s — a formula said
aloud to make something happen — and the difference from
[saiyasat](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual) is not the mechanism. It is the audience.

## What gets chanted, and why — the origin stories

The chants of the *paritta* set are not abstract protections. Each one carries
a story explaining what it once saved, and
[Supreme Maha Mantra Katha](/m/supreme-maha-mantra-katha-6968/) — a contributed
printed volume with full transcription and translation — sets them out one after
another. Read three of them and the whole logic of protective chanting becomes
obvious.

**The Karaniya Metta Sutta** (p.103) begins as a haunting. Monks take leave of
the Buddha to spend the rains retreat in a forest. The *rukkha-devatā*, the tree
spirits, come down to the ground to pay their respects, assuming the visit will
be brief. It is not brief. Living on the ground becomes inconvenient, and since
they cannot move higher, they resort to frightening the monks out. The monks
retreat to the Buddha, who does not exorcise anything; he sends them back with
a chant of loving-kindness, and when the devas hear it, loving-kindness arises
in them and the haunting stops. *Inference —* this is the founding case of the
whole northern protective repertoire, and it is a **negotiation**, not an
expulsion. The problem is a housing dispute; the solution is good manners
addressed to the party with the grievance. Set it beside the *mae sü* courtesy
in the [amulet manuals](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual) and the same etiquette is
running in both.

**The Mora Paritta** (p.104) is a Bodhisatta-as-golden-peacock story with an
unusually harsh ending. The peacock recites the chant morning and evening
before going out to feed, and a hunter lies in wait for him for **seven years**
without ever taking him. Then a second hunter changes tactics: he brings a hen
peacock as a lure, the peacock is so distracted he forgets to recite — and is
caught at once. The protection never failed. The reciter did.

**The Vaṭṭaka Paritta** (p.105), the "quail chant," is the shortest and the
best. A forest fire is running; every animal flees, including the little
quail's parents; the fire is about to reach the nest. The chick cannot fly and
cannot walk, so it makes a *sacca-adhiṭṭhāna*, an asseveration of truth, and
what it says is simply the truth of its own helplessness: *I have wings but
cannot fly, I have feet but cannot walk; my parents have fled. Let the flames
retreat.* They retreat. The words that became the fire-protection chant are a
statement of total inability — which is a strange and rather beautiful thing
for a power-formula to be made of.

Alongside these the same volume records the practical end of the repertoire —
the **Aṅgulimāla Paritta** recited over water for a woman in labour, or by the
labouring woman herself, so that she may give birth easily (p.105); the
**Bojjhaṅga Paritta**, chanted to a sick man who recovers at that very moment
(p.105); the **Jinapañjara**, which invites the 28 Buddhas and the eighty great
disciples to take up residence in the reciter's own body and stand around him
(p.104); the **Khandha Paritta**, which protects against venomous creatures by
spreading loving-kindness to venomous creatures. And it answers the layperson's
question directly, in a section titled *What Is Chanting For?* (p.110–111),
where the compiler simply narrates her own life: chanting before bed as a child
in imitation of her grandfather; later, in the capital, with problems she had
nowhere to take, chanting as the one thing to hold onto; the Jinapañjara learnt
by heart and carried on every journey; ordination as a *mae chi*, twice;
morning chanting and an hour of sitting, every day, for almost ten years. Her
theory of the practice is unsentimental — chanting is *samatha*, an anchor for
the mind in one place, a way of fully recharging so it can rise into vipassanā;
and if you occasionally chant it wrong, there is no need to worry.

## The Uppatasanti — Lanna's own great chant

One text in the corpus is described in the treatises as specifically and
anciently northern. The **Uppatasanti** (อุปปาทสันติ), which
[Supreme Maha Mantra Katha](/m/supreme-maha-mantra-katha-6968/) (p.102) says
"has been widely known among the Lanna people since ancient times, called the
*Great Santing Luang*," is a **271-verse** roll-call of virtue: 28 Buddhas of
the past, one of the future, the Pacceka Buddhas, the nine supramundane states,
108 senior elders and then thirteen more, the nāga kings, certain classes of
hungry ghost, the asuras, forty-one verses of devas, sixteen of brahmās, and at
the end ten verses on the pacification of calamity itself. It is recited to
invoke good fortune, drive off evil, overpower an enemy's strength, guard
against non-human beings, and — the phrase is worth keeping — as *a Dhamma to
escape death before its appointed time.*

The catalogue holds **five witnesses**, and where they are filed is the
finding. Two are here under liturgy's neighbours — ms 3124 (Wat Phra That Chang
Kham, palm-leaf, 1927) and ms 3125 (Wat Na Pang) under
[magic and ritual](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual); ms 2560 (Wat Sung Men, palm-leaf,
1767) and ms 3127 (Wat Muang Tuet, 1925) under
[astrology](/a?s=genre:astrology); ms 3126 (Wat Hia, 1903) under
[Buddhist canonical](/a?s=genre:buddhist_canonical). *Inference —* one text,
five copies, four different genre labels. The taxonomy is not failing; the text
genuinely is a canonical recitation, a protective working, and a calendrical
instrument at once, and each cataloguer filed the face they saw.

The **Mahāsamaya Sutta** does the same trick at larger scale — 16 witnesses
across the corpus, five of them in this genre, the rest mostly canonical. The
treatise's own gloss (p.102) explains why a sutta that is simply a very long
list of arriving devas became a favourite performance piece: *when this text is
recited, the devas are pleased to listen because each of them is named.* It is
a chant that works by roll-call, and the guest list is the point. See
[Mahāsamaya](/a?s=entity:mahasamai).

## The funeral, counted out in monks

The clearest picture of a northern liturgy in operation comes from
[Setting Up Ceremonies, Ancient Edition](/m/setting-up-ceremonies-ancient-edition-6969/),
which sorts every ceremony into two classes on its first working page (p.3):
*mangala*, the auspicious, and the inauspicious — and then gives each its own
physics.

**The sacred thread runs in opposite directions** (p.5). For an auspicious
rite, the *sai sin* begins at the Buddha image or the household shrine, loops
**clockwise** around the building and the ceremony ground, returns to circle
the image's base once or three times, and its coil is laid on a tray to the
left of the altar. For an inauspicious rite, the thread runs from the **corpse**
— or from the urn of ashes, or from the portrait of the deceased, or from the
written list of the dead's names — directly to the monks, for the *bangsukul*.
*Inference —* the same physical cord is the instrument in both cases; what
changes is which end of it a body is attached to.

**And the monks are counted** (p.6). Ten for a royal or government auspicious
rite; five for any ceremony held by the general public, weddings included. For
a funeral, royal and commoner alike:

- **4 monks** to chant the **Abhidhamma**
- **4 monks** to chant before the cremation fire
- 5, 7, 9 or 10 to chant the blessing texts, according to one's faith
- 20, 25, 50, 100 or 500 — or the entire monastery — for the *sawotchaeng*
- and for the *mātikā-bangsukul*, **a number of monks equal to the age of the
  deceased**

That last line is the one to carry away. A life is converted directly into a
quantity of recitation. *Tradition holds —* the Abhidhamma at a Thai funeral is
famously not chanted for the mourners' comprehension; the seven books are
recited as the densest available concentration of Dhamma, offered on behalf of
the dead, and the north's *mātikā* — the bare list of Abhidhamma headings — is
the version most often heard over a coffin. The archive's palm-leaf shelf
carries the northern verses that go with it: ms 432 at Wat Hia includes
***คำไหว้อภิธัมมาเจ็ดกัมพี***, the words of homage to the seven books of the
Abhidhamma, bound beside a meditation manual and a Buddha-veneration text.

The same treatise then descends, without changing tone, into modern
administration (p.147): report a death at a hospital and collect the medical
certificate; report a death at home to the registrar, the sub-district doctor,
the headman or the *kamnan* within 24 hours; in an accident or a homicide, call
the police and do not move the body. The ceremonial handbook and the civil
registry are the same book.

## The shape of the collection

Liturgy is written overwhelmingly in **Tham Lanna** — 91 of 97 — but its
language profile is the most distinctive in the archive: **15 witnesses are
monolingual Pali**, needing no vernacular at all, because the chant *is* the
Pali and a reciter who has the melody does not need a gloss. Three are Pali and
Lao in Tham Lao script, all from Wat Sung Men and all dated 1836; three are
Pali and Shan, including ms 4437 at [Wat Pa Pao](/place/wat-pa-pao/) in Chiang
Mai (khoi paper, 1916) and two at Wat Si Khom Kham in Phayao. For a genre of 97,
that is four liturgical languages sharing one shelf.

Its support leans harder to **paper (28 of 97)** than the scholastic genres do,
exactly as one would expect of a text opened and read during a performance
rather than shelved. It is spread unusually evenly across the upper north —
Phrae 21, Lampang 20, Nan 19, then Chiang Mai 11 and Phayao 10 — with
[Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/) far in front at 18 witnesses and
[Wat Lai Hin Luang](/place/wat-lai-hin-luang/) second at 7.

And it reaches back further than anything else in the *wichaa* orbit. **56 of
the 97 are dated, running 1541 to 1964** — the oldest being ms 4369,
*Pali sut hom*, from Wat Lai Hin Luang, in monolingual Pali on palm-leaf,
**1541**. The same title appears at the same temple twice more (ms 4632,
ms 4644), also monolingual Pali, undated. *Inference —* three copies of one
Pali-only recitation text kept in one Lampang library, with the earliest dated
witness in this genre among them, is what a **working choir's music cupboard**
looks like in the archaeological record: not one revered exemplar, but spare
parts.

## The saiyasat hinge — why this genre matters to the wichaa

This is the genre where canonical Buddhism and efficacious *saiyasat* are **the
same performance seen from two sides**. Seven titles here name a
***thon*** — the recitation that un-does — and they are the working half of the
genre: ms 204 at Wat Phra That Chang Kham (1907) binds the *suat boek* to the
withdrawal for a dead man, for a dead woman, and for all the dead together;
ms 363 at Wat Si Mongkhon (1932) does it for the house and the home; ms 521 at
Wat Pa Mueat pairs the method of building a house with the sending-off and
withdrawal of *sappa khuet*, all inauspiciousness, and the *kalakini* of the
dwelling. ms 537 at Wat Pa Mueat and ms 2841 at Wat Klang each bind *sut mon*
directly to [holasat](/a?s=entity:holasat) — the chant and the almanac in one
folding book. See [thon](/a?s=entity:sut_thon), which follows this rite across
both genres.

The [Magic & Ritual](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual) article describes the
benedictory, consecratory end of saiyasat. This genre *is* that end, filed
under "Chanting" because the technology is a spoken formula and the setting is
a vihara full of people. For this archive's esoteric priorities the liturgy is
not a backwater — it is where the protective core lives in its most public,
communal, and entirely unembarrassed form.

There is one telling asymmetry. Every one of the 388
[astrology](/a?s=genre:astrology) witnesses and all 276
[magic-ritual](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual) witnesses are flagged research-priority
— but **only 6 of these 97 are**. *Inference —* the scorer is reading titles,
and a title that says *Sut mon* looks canonical while a title that says
*Sainyasat* does not. On the evidence of this page, that is the wrong reading
of what a *sut thon sappakhuet* actually does, and re-scoring the liturgy shelf
is a live task rather than a hypothetical one.

## Notes

- **Read it beside saiyasat, and check for hybrids.** The *thon sappakhuet*,
  *kalakini* and *sut-mon-with-holasat* volumes straddle the liturgy /
  [magic](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual) boundary; when a chanting title also names a
  protective or calendrical element, check the raw metadata — the object is
  usually a household ritual manual, not pure liturgy.
- **Heavily funerary and occasional.** The *thon* for the dead, the house and
  misfortune are the practical working half of this genre — the liturgy of the
  life-cycle. Splitting the *paritta* and *sut* from the *thon* from the
  *suat boek* would separate three quite different performance settings and is
  the obvious next taxonomy pass.
- **The soul-callings are next door, not here.** Only **one** liturgy title
  names a *khwan* — ms 367 at Wat Si Mongkhon, *Sut lae su khwan cang*,
  chanting bound with the **soul-calling of an elephant**. The other 32
  soul-calling witnesses are filed under
  [magic and ritual](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual); see
  [su khwan](/a?s=entity:su_khwan) and the archive's own living
  [su khwan for machines](/sukhwan).
- **Linked to the birth-stories by performance.** The invitation to preach the
  Vessantara — *Kam alathana tham wetsantala*, ms 370 at Wat Si Mongkhon — ties
  this genre to [jātaka](/a?s=genre:jataka) at the Thet Mahachat: the same text
  is a birth-story on one shelf and a recitation cue on another. ms 673 at
  Wat Mueang Ram (monolingual Pali, 1893) sets out the fifteen-day cycle "from
  *Yesanta* through to the Mahāsamaya Sutta" — a fortnight of chanting written
  down as an itinerary.
- **Where to go next.** [Browse the genre](/browse?genre=liturgy_chanting) to
  see the 97 in provenance order; the [glossary](/glossary/) holds the emic
  vocabulary these titles use; [find by need](/need) reaches the same rites from
  the other side, by what a household came wanting.
