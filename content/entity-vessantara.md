## What this is

The **Vessantara** — the *Mahāchat*, มหาชาติ, "the Great Birth" — is the last of
the ten final lives of the Buddha-to-be, and the most copied story in this
archive. It is the story of a prince who gives away everything, in the wrong order,
to the wrong people, and is right to. Lanna did not merely preserve it. Lanna
recited it once a year in a marathon that took a full day, split it into thirteen
sponsorable chapters so that thirteen families could each own one, wrote out at
least **sixty-nine named performance versions** of it, and then carried its
syllables into the magical curriculum, where the whole birth-tale is compressed
to four letters you can chant over your own flesh.

This page tells the story first, because the catalogue makes no sense without it.

## The story, first

*Tradition holds —* it opens with a **white elephant that makes rain**. Prince
Vessantara of Sivi is born with his hand out — his first words, the tradition
says, are a request for something to give away — and grows up practising *dāna*,
generosity, as an athlete practises a lift. His kingdom owns a white elephant that
brings the monsoon. A delegation arrives from a neighbouring kingdom in drought
and asks for it. He gives it to them.

The city riots. An elephant that makes rain is not a possession, it is the state's
water supply, and the prince has handed it to foreigners because they asked
politely. The citizens force the king to exile him. Vessantara accepts, asks for
one day to complete the **Gift of the Seven Hundreds** — seven hundred each of
elephants, horses, chariots, women, cattle, slaves, and vessels of wine — empties
the treasury, and leaves in a carriage with his wife **Maddī** and their two small
children, **Jāli** and **Kaṇhā**. On the road brahmins ask for the horses; he gives
them. One asks for the carriage; he gives it. The family walks the rest of the way
into the Himavanta forest and builds two leaf huts.

Then the story does the thing that has kept people arguing about it for two
thousand years. An old brahmin named **Jūjaka** — grasping, ugly, and married to a
young wife who is tired of drawing her own water — walks into the forest to ask
for the children as slaves. He times it for a day when Maddī is out gathering
fruit, because he knows the mother will not allow it. Vessantara gives away Jāli
and Kaṇhā. The children hide in a lotus pond; their father calls them out; Jūjaka
beats them in front of him and drives them off tied at the wrists, and Vessantara
sits with his hand on his sword and does not move, because the perfection he is
completing is the perfection of not taking back. Maddī comes home at dusk to an
empty hut and searches the forest all night before he tells her. The next morning
a disguised Indra arrives and asks for Maddī. He gives her too — and Indra
immediately gives her back, which is the story admitting that this last gift was a
test and the previous one was not.

*Tradition holds —* it ends in reunion and it is not a comfortable one. Jūjaka,
steering by greed, blunders into the very city the children were born in;
their grandfather recognises them and ransoms them at the extravagant price Jāli
had set on his own head; Jūjaka eats himself to death at the celebration feast.
The exile is lifted, the six royals meet in the forest and faint together — the
chapter called *Chakkhattiya*, "the six nobles" — and the family goes home. But
nobody in the audience has forgotten the pond, and the recitation does not ask
them to. **The point of the Great Birth is not that generosity feels good. It is
that the last perfection before Buddhahood costs more than a listener would pay.**

*Inference —* that discomfort is exactly why it became the communal text rather
than a private one. A story this hard is not read alone; it is performed, out
loud, in a building full of people, once a year, with the hardest chapters
sponsored by name.

## What the catalogue holds

**Eighty-five manuscripts carry *Wetsantala* in the title.** Their shape is
canonical and old: **80 of 85 palm-leaf**, **84 of 85 Tham Lanna**, 69 of them
dated, running **1646 to 1951**. Seventy-six sit under
[jātaka](/a?s=genre:jataka); the rest scatter into
[Buddhist canonical](/a?s=genre:buddhist_canonical) (4),
[magic & ritual](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual) (3),
[poetry](/a?s=genre:poetry_literary) (1) and
[liturgy](/a?s=genre:liturgy_chanting) (1). Geographically it does **not** behave
like plain jātaka, which piles up in the great scriptorium at
[Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/): here **Lampang leads with 42 against
Phrae's 25**, and within Lampang a single village temple, Wat Ban Luk, holds 24
copies on its own.

But 85 badly undercounts it, and the reason is instructive. **Lanna scribes
usually did not write "Vessantara" on the bundle. They wrote the chapter names.**
A typical Wat Sung Men title runs: *Tatsapon, Himmapan, Tanakhan, Wannapawet,
Cucok, Cunlapon, Mahapon, Sakkaban, Mahalat, Sakkati, Nakon* — eleven of the
thirteen chapters listed in order, with no work-title at all, because everyone
knew. Count manuscripts whose titles either name Vessantara **or** list two or
more chapters of his cycle and the figure rises to **182**, of which **172 are
filed as jātaka — roughly one in nine of the 1,633-manuscript
[jātaka shelf](/browse?genre=jataka)**. On that count the province
order flips back: **Phrae 76, Lampang 67, Nan 18**, with 58 at
[Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/) alone. One diagnostic name alone does most of
the work: **74 manuscripts name ชูชก Jūjaka in the title.** The beggar who takes
the children is, by title-frequency, the most famous character in Lanna's
manuscript literature.

## Thirteen chapters, thirteen books

The thirteen *kan* — ทศพร *Thatsaphon* (the ten boons), หิมพานต์ *Himaphan* (the
Himavanta forest), ทานกัณฑ์ *Thanakhan* (the great giving), วนประเวศน์
*Wannapawet* (entering the wood), ชูชก *Jūjaka*, จุลพน *Cunlaphon* (the lesser
forest), มหาพน *Mahāphon* (the greater forest), กุมาร *Kumān* (the children),
มัทรี *Maddī*, สักกบรรพ *Sakkabap* (Indra's chapter), มหาราช *Mahārāt* (the great
king), ฉกษัตริย์ *Chakkhattiya* (the six nobles), นครกัณฑ์ *Nakhon* (the return
to the city) — are not just an internal division. **In this catalogue they are
physically separate objects.** Beyond the composite bundles, the catalogue holds
witnesses whose titles name one chapter and nothing else: **ชูชก *Cucok***, ms
4048, [Wat Bun Yuen](/place/wat-bun-yuen-nan/) in Nan, 1884, eight leaves;
**มัทธี *Matthi***, ms 4791, [Wat Chedi Luang](/place/wat-chedi-luang-chiang-mai/)
in Chiang Mai, six leaves; **มหาพล *Mahapon***, ms 5119, Wat Wiang in Lampang,
1759, five leaves; **มหาราช *Mahalat*** twice over at
[Wat Lai Hin Luang](/place/wat-lai-hin-luang/) in Lampang, one of them dated 1722.
Five leaves is not a book. It is a part. *Inference —* that is
what a sponsorship economy leaves behind in a library. If your family's obligation
at the festival is the Maddī chapter, what you commission, own, lend and store is
the Maddī chapter, and the great composite bundles are the temple's master
copies rather than the working ones.

The order in the titles is not always the printed order, either. Ms 5008 at Wat
Ban Ueam (Lampang, 1872) lists **Cucok, Mahapon, Culapon** — the two forest
chapters transposed. Ms 5006 (1860, same temple) drops Jūjaka's chapter entirely
and keeps the rest. *Inference —* these are performance sets, and a set is missing
a chapter because that year somebody else had it.

## Sixty-nine editions with names

The single most striking thing in the Vessantara data is a habit of the Lanna
colophon: the bundle ends with **ฉบับ + a name**, "the version of ___." Of the 182
Vessantara-cycle manuscripts, **83 carry such a tag, and they use 69 distinct
names between them.** These are not edition numbers. They are groves, villages,
persons and jokes:

- **ฉบับเถ้าลืมตื่น** *sabap thao luem tuen* — "the old man who forgot to wake"
  (ms 1666, [Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/), 1877)
- **ฉบับวิงวอน** *sabap wing won*, "entreaty" — with a whole family around it:
  ฉบับวิงวอนดอนแก้ว (ms 5006, Wat Ban Ueam, Lampang, 1860),
  ฉบับวิงวอนดอนกลาง (ms 5312, Wat Ban Luk, Lampang, 1787),
  ฉบับวิงวอนดอนกางเชียงใหม่ (ms 1751, Wat Sung Men, 1801), and the splendid
  ฉบับวิงวอนมวนอาระขะเก้าหน้า (ms 2663, Wat Phra Luang, Phrae)
- **the forest versions** — ฉบับป่ากล้วย "banana grove" (ms 2731, Wat Mueang Mo,
  1910), ฉบับป่าไผ่ "bamboo grove" (ms 3644, Wat Kittiwong, Mae Hong Son),
  ฉบับป่าเหียง (ms 2712, 1872), ฉบับป่าฝาง (ms 2718, 1840), ฉบับป่าชาง
  (ms 4856, [Wat Phra That Lampang Luang](/place/wat-phra-that-lampang-luang/),
  1805), ฉบับแม่กุป่าหลิน (ms 5358, Wat Ban Luk, 1791)
- **ฉบับพันนาเชียงรุ้ง** — Phanna Chiang Rung, which is to say **Sipsongpanna**,
  the Lue country up the Mekong. Eight manuscripts carry it, spelled four
  different ways (พันนาเชียงรุ่ง, พันนาเชียงรุ้ง, พันนาเชียรุ้ง, พันนาเชืยงรุ้ง), from
  1839 to 1909, at [Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/) and Wat Mueang Mo in Phrae
  and Wat Hang Chat and Wat Ban Ueam in Lampang. *Inference —* a foreign
  house-version that four scribal communities all thought worth naming, and none
  of them agreed how to spell.
- **ฉบับพระยอดส้อยเมืองคลางเชียงไหม** and **ฉบับพระสิงหลวงเมืองเชืยงไหม่** — two
  Chiang Mai versions, copied out at Wat Ban Ueam in Lampang (ms 5008, 1872) and
  Wat Klang in Phrae (ms 2812, 1916), neither of them anywhere near Chiang Mai
- **ฉบับโสฬสสิบหกเมือง** — "the sixteen mueang," twice over in one phrase, Pali and
  Thai (ms 1793, [Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/), 1913)
- **ฉบับตลุง และทะโค่ง** — a *Himaphan* chapter tagged with two recension names at
  once, with the *Cucok* in the same bundle tagged with the second of them alone
  (ms 622, Wat Na Pang, Nan, 1888)
- **ฉบับลวงฅำ** *sabap luang kham*, the most travelled: Wat Sung Men in 1870 and
  again in 1890, Wat Don Chai Phra Bat in Nan in 1891, Wat Mueang Mo in 1905 —
  one named version crossing two provinces over thirty-five years

*Inference —* a text does not accumulate sixty-nine named versions by being read.
It accumulates them by being **performed** — these are the recitation styles,
melodies and house-texts of a competitive annual event, and the ฉบับ tag is the
scribe recording which one this copy sings. This is the single richest untapped
index in the catalogue: a ฉบับ facet would let a reader trace one house-version
across temples and decades, which is a question no title search can currently
ask.

## The apparatus for putting it on

The Vessantara does not travel alone. Around the birth-story the catalogue holds
the whole working kit of the **Thet Mahachat** festival, and each piece belongs to
a different genre — which is why this entity is the archive's best demonstration
that the genre walls are porous.

**The invitation.** *Kam alathana tham wetsantala*, คำอาราธนาธัมม์เวสสันตระ — the
*ārādhanā* words that formally invite a monk to preach the Great Birth. There are
**four** in the catalogue and, tellingly, **all four come from Nan**: ms 314 and
ms 370 from Wat Si Mongkhon (Kong) — the same formula catalogued once as
[magic & ritual](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual) and once as
[liturgy](/a?s=genre:liturgy_chanting), a filing disagreement that is itself the
argument of this page — and ms 492 and ms 516 from Wat Pa Mueat. Look at what
ms 516 is bound with: **the Vessantara invitation, the words for
[calling the khwan](/a?s=entity:su_khwan), and a
[sut thon](/a?s=entity:sut_thon)**, all in one bundle. A fifth *ārādhanā*
manuscript from the same district, ms 416 at Wat Hia, binds a different invitation
— the one for the Rains Retreat — in beside a
[suep cata](/a?s=entity:suep_cata) life-extension procedure. *Inference —* these
are not scripture volumes. They are an officiant's working folder: the four or
five things a village ritual specialist actually gets asked to do.

**The merit-accounting.** *Anisong wetsantala*, อานิสงส์เวสสันตระ — the
*ānisaṃsa* that enumerates what a listener earns by hearing the whole recitation
in a single day. **Twenty-five witnesses** name it. Some stand alone — ms 5439 at
Wat Ban Luk (Lampang, 1782), ms 3832 at
[Wat Si Khom Kham](/place/wat-si-khom-kham-phayao/) (Phayao, 1860), ms 1252 at
Wat Kittiwong (Mae Hong Son, 1704) — but most sit at the **end** of a full
thirteen-chapter bundle, after *Nakhon kan*, exactly where a reckoning of the
day's merit belongs when the day is over.

**The thousand verses.** ms 2714 at Wat Mueang Mo (Phrae, 1905) closes its bundle
with **สหัสสะคาถามหาเวสสันตระ**, the *Sahassa-gāthā* — the "thousand verses," the
Pali skeleton of the whole tale that a preacher chants through as the spine of the
performance. The printed
[biography of Luang Por Thob](/m/life-luang-por-thob-katha-he-used-6974/) (p.6)
confirms it was still a working skill in the twentieth century. Having taken the
full transmission of incantation-lore from his two teachers, the young Luang Por
Thob went on to "practise chanting the Mahachat sermon, in both the **คาถาพัน**
verse-formulas and the **กัณฑ์ราย** sermon-chapters, in the style favoured by
people of that era, most beautifully" — the same two-part technique the Phrae
manuscript preserves, listed by a Central Thai devotional biography as one of a
famous magician-monk's accomplishments, alongside the katha.

**The night before.** Bundles again and again put **มาลัยหมื่น มาลัยแสน**, the
Malai texts, immediately before the Vessantara chapters — ms 1639 at Wat Sung Men
(1811) lists *Malai muen malai saen* right after *Nakhon*; ms 1749 and ms 2710
sandwich *Pathama malai* and *Tutiya malai* between the last chapter and the
*anisong*. *Tradition holds —* Phra Malai's tour of the hells and the heavens is
recited on the eve of Thet Mahachat, and it is Malai who brings back Metteyya's
condition for meeting him: hear the entire Vessantara in one day. *Inference —*
the binding order in these bundles is the running order of the festival.

**The version for fun.** ms 556, **คำเหล้นคร่าวเวสสันตระ** *Kam len khao
wetsantala*, from [Wat Bun Yuen](/place/wat-bun-yuen-nan/) in Nan — the *khao*, a
Lanna verse form, "words for
*playing* the Vessantara." Filed under [poetry](/a?s=genre:poetry_literary),
because that is what it is. The same story that earns a listener a shot at the
next Buddha is also the one you set to a tune and enjoy.

**And the version filed as history.** ms 3451, **ตำนานเวสสันตระ** *Tamnan
wetsantala*, Wat Kasa, Chiang Rai, 1930 — a scribe who reached for
[*tamnan*](/a?s=genre:tamnan_chronicle), the word for a chronicle, and used it for
a birth-tale. (The cataloguer put it back on the jātaka shelf.) Five registers,
then, not four: scripture, liturgical cue, merit-manual, folk-song, and — for at
least one copyist in Chiang Rai — history.

## Vessantara inside the wichaa

Where the Great Birth gets genuinely strange is on the magical shelf, where the
whole thirteen-chapter epic is treated as a **power with a heart-syllable**, like
a god or a weapon.

Two separate printed treatises, from different lineages, carry the same
curriculum table: a column of syllables against a column of what they compress.
In the [Vedic katha for chai chatri](/m/warrior-vedic-katha-chai-chatri-6972/)
(p.51) it reads **สะระตะนะ — พระเวสสันดร**; in the
[Khom-Thai inscription formulas](/m/yantra-inscription-formulas-mixed-khom-thai-6977/)
(p.21) it reads **สะระนะตะ — หัวใจพระเวสสันดร**, "the heart of Vessantara," two
letters transposed by one copyist or the other. In both books the neighbourhood is
the same — the Boiling Ganges sits directly above it — and in the fuller of the two
the rest of the final lives run on down the column: Vidhura, Mahosadha, Temiya,
**Bhūridatta** the [naga](/a?s=entity:naga)-prince, Suvaṇṇasāma, Mahājanaka. Ten
whole birth-tales as a set of four-syllable keys, each a jātaka folded into a
chant. *Inference —* on this
shelf a birth-tale is not a text you read. It is a battery.

The [Compendium of Ancient Katha](/m/compendium-katha-from-old-manuals-6966/)
shows what that looks like at full length. Its **Great Assembly
invocation** — the one whose closing note reads *"this katha, the great assembly
of devas, is said before performing any working at all"* — runs on for pages,
summoning everything there is: the future Buddha Metteyya, Kaccāyana,
Aṅgulimāla, Ānanda, the seven planets and Rāhu, Shiva and Vishnu, the sixteen
Brahma heavens by name, the rivers, the monkey army of the Rāmāyaṇa — and, in the
middle of it, the Ten Births one by one, arriving at **"Phra Vessantara and Queen
Maddī, the children Kaṇhā and Jāli, the Great Bodhi Tree, and the excellent heart
born of generosity."** The family is invoked as a unit, children included, as
though they were a deity with a retinue.

And then the best sentence about the Great Birth in the entire corpus, from the
same book (p.25). The **คาถาปลุกเครื่อง**, the katha for awakening the body and
its equipment, walks down the practitioner's own anatomy converting each part into
armour: hair to diamond fur, twenty nails to bronze, thirty-two teeth to magic
iron, skin to an amulet-vest, nine hundred great tendons to jewelled cloth, bones
to kapok — and, in the middle of the list:

**"มังสัง — my flesh, all three hundred pieces, becomes the Great Birth Tale
(มหาชาติ), granting invulnerability."**

*Inference —* nothing else in the archive states the Vessantara's standing so
economically. Every other item on that list is a substance — metal, stone, cloth,
fibre. In the flesh slot, where you would expect iron, the treatise puts a
**story**. The Great Birth is what the practitioner's body is made of.

Two smaller sightings complete the set. The
[Phetcharat great yantra manual](/m/phetcharat-great-yantra-manual-6960/) (p.76)
prints a square yant with a diamond inscribed inside and four corner cells of
Khom, and glosses it: *"This Phra Kala image is **Phra Vessantara**"* — the prince
as the identity of a [yantra](/a?s=entity:yantra), with instructions to consecrate
it "so that the mouth becomes a charm of attraction." *Inference —* a figure whose
defining act is giving without limit makes obvious sense as a persuasion-charm:
what he is famous for is being unable to say no.

## Jūjaka goes to market

The strangest afterlife belongs to the villain. On the archive's
[market layer](/w/products) — the 13,020-listing Lazada snapshot — **eight
listings name ชูชก Jūjaka**, and none of them treats him as a cautionary tale.
He is a **wealth charm**. There is a *เหรียญโภคทรัพย์* prosperity coin with
**Nang Kwak the beckoning lady on the front and Jūjaka on the reverse**, listed as
หลวงปู่อั๊บ, วัดท้องไทร, Nakhon Pathom, 2552 BE. There is a second Nang Kwak coin
"backed with Grandfather Jūjaka, the Great Fortune, Great Millionaire edition."
There is a cast figure of him attributed to หลวงปู่ทิม at ฿500. There are two
[hun payont](/hun) effigies of Jūjaka in *lek lai* metal at ฿90 each. There is a
silver piece packed with the prai powder of fifty-nine spirits, showing
**Jūjaka paired with นางอมิตดา, his young wife**, at ฿5,500. And there is
**น้ำมันชูชก**, "Jūjaka oil," sold under the frank subtitle **น้ำมันขอเมีย** —
"the oil for asking for a wife" — at ฿550.

*Inference —* the market has read the story with total accuracy and no
sentiment whatsoever. Jūjaka is the man in the tale who **asks for the
impossible and gets it**, and who ends up with a young wife he did nothing to
deserve. Strip the morality out and that is a functional specification. The
Great Birth's whole moral architecture depends on Vessantara being the one you
admire — and the amulet trade, needing a patron of successful asking, went
straight past him to the brahmin at the door.

## Notes

- **Numbers on this page, and what they count.** *85* is manuscripts with
  *Wetsantala* in the title; *182* is manuscripts naming Vessantara **or** listing
  two or more chapters of his cycle; *74* name Jūjaka; *69* is distinct ฉบับ
  recension names; *25* carry an *anisong*; *4* carry the Vessantara *ārādhanā*
  invitation.
  The 85-set's profile (76 jātaka, 80 palm-leaf, 1646–1951, Lampang 42 / Phrae 25)
  was checked against the catalogue and stands as previously published.
- **ฉบับ is the facet this archive is missing.** Sixty-nine named versions across
  83 manuscripts is a performance tradition hiding inside a metadata field. *Inference —* extracting
  ฉบับ into a browsable facet would be a small parse over existing titles and
  would open a question — which house-version travelled where — that nothing on
  the site can currently answer. It is the highest-value cheap index in the
  catalogue.
- **Chapter-level indexing is now demonstrably worth it.** Because the thirteen
  *kan* have stable names and appear as standalone witnesses, a *Jūjaka* facet or
  a *Maddī* facet is buildable from titles alone, and would surface dozens of
  manuscripts that the current entity search does not connect to this page at
  all.
- **Read it beside the rite pages.** The [su khwan](/a?s=entity:su_khwan),
  [sut thon](/a?s=entity:sut_thon) and [suep cata](/a?s=entity:suep_cata) entities
  share physical bundles with the Vessantara invitation in Nan; the
  [katha](/a?s=entity:katha) and [yantra](/a?s=entity:yantra) pages hold the forms
  its heart-syllable is filed under; and the [naga](/a?s=entity:naga) page follows
  Bhūridatta, who stands four names away from Vessantara in the same invocation
  and, like him, is famous for refusing to take anything back.
