## What this is

The **naga** (นาค; Pali *nāga*) is the water-serpent of Southeast Asian cosmology
— lord of the rivers and of the treasure under the ground, storm-bringer and
rain-giver, and in the Buddhist frame a being of enormous power who chose to
kneel. In the Lanna world he is not one thing. He is a character in a birth-tale,
a shape you draw with a particular opening syllable, a candle you roll to cancel
a bad year, an alloy you beat into a takrut, a name your family calls you on the
morning you ordain, and a stone balustrade you put your hand on climbing to a
shrine. This page follows him through all of those — the story first, then the
witnesses, then the working naga in the printed manuals, where he turns out to be
far busier than a title search suggests.

## The story, first

*Tradition holds —* the founding image is the naga who did not strike. In the
weeks after the awakening, a storm comes down on the Buddha sitting under a
mucalinda tree, and the serpent-king **Mucalinda** rises out of the roots, winds
his coils seven times around the seated body and spreads his hood over the head
like a roof, holding the rain off for seven days. Then, the story says, he
unwinds, takes the form of a young man, and stands before the Buddha with his
hands together. Everything else the naga does in this tradition is a footnote to
that gesture: enormous force, voluntarily bent to shelter. It is why the coiled
serpent with the fanned hood is carved behind Buddha images, why he runs down
every temple stair-rail with the whole flight of steps disappearing into his
mouth, and why he rears up as the ช่อฟ้า finial at the roof-ridge — the building
itself held in a serpent's grip, protectively.

*Tradition holds —* the second story is why a Thai man is called a naga on the
day he ordains. In the Vinaya's telling, a naga so admired the monastic life that
he took human shape and got himself ordained; asleep in his hut, he relaxed back
into his true body, and a monk who came to wake him found the room full of
serpent. The Buddha sent him away — an animal cannot hold the training — and the
naga wept and asked that his name at least be kept. It was. **Every candidate for
ordination in Thailand is still called นาค.** He is shaved as a naga, dressed in
white as a naga, carried around the ordination hall as a naga, and only stops
being one at the moment the robes go on. The most ordinary rite in the Thai
calendar is built on a serpent's disappointment.

*Tradition holds —* the third naga is the working one. He governs water, so he
governs the year: the old almanacs open with a count of how many nagas will
"give water" — one naga means a heavy year, seven or eight means a thin one, and
the reckoning inverts the way a westerner expects, because the more of them share
the labour the less each one pours. He sleeps beneath the ground in a slow annual
circuit, and his head-and-tail alignment tells a builder which way to cut the
first post, a gravedigger which way to lay the body, and a householder which
direction not to dig this season — get it backwards and you have driven a spade
into the serpent's face. On the Mekong he is **พญานาค Phaya Nak**, whose fireballs
rise off the river at the end of the Rains Retreat, and whose household on the
Thai side has taken names and personalities — Pu Si Sutho and Ya Si Pathum of the
Kham Chanot grove chief among them. That last register is modern, popular, and
booming, and it shows up in this archive's market data below.

*Inference —* hold those three together and the shape of the evidence stops
being surprising. The naga of story sits in the [jātaka](/a?s=genre:jataka) shelf
under his own name. The naga of *wichaa* does not get titles, because he is never
the subject of a manual — he is an ingredient inside one. So a title search finds
the first and misses the second almost entirely, and the printed treatises are
where the second one is waiting.

## The witnesses that carry his name

**Nine manuscripts in the catalogue put a naga in the title.** They fall into
four small clusters; eight of the nine are in Tham Lanna, and seven are on palm
leaf.

- **มหานาควัตถุ *Mahanak watthu*, "the Matter of the Great Naga"** — three
  copies, all from **Wat Kittiwong** in Mae Sariang district, **Mae Hong Son**:
  ms 1271 (1713, 9 folios, "slightly damaged," dated in the colophon by the
  year-name *pi ka sai*), ms 1283 (1722), and ms 1270 (1733), this last one
  catalogued as *Untitled* with the title supplied in brackets. The source classes
  all three as นิยายธรรม, "Buddhist tale." **And they are probably not about a
  serpent at all.** Each carries a second, older PLMP cataloguing title underneath
  the modern one, and the three disagree: ms 1270 says มหานาควัตถุ, agreeing;
  ms 1283 says **มหานาคเถรวัตถุ**, "the Matter of *the Elder* Mahānāga"; and
  ms 1271 says **มหาพกุลเถรวัตถุ**, the story of the elder Mahābakkula, a different
  monk entirely. *Inference —* Mahānāga is an ordinary Pali monastic name — the
  same joke the ordination hall plays, one register up — and two of the three
  cards read this as a monk's life. The archive's largest apparent naga cluster is
  its least certain, and reading nine folios would settle it. Until someone does,
  count these three softly.
- **ปุณณะนาคกุมมาน / สุปุณณะนาค *Punnanak kumman* and *Supunnanak*** — four
  witnesses of what looks like one work and its variant: ms 3305 (1881) and
  ms 3306 (1910), both without recorded provenance; ms 3307 at **Wat Ban Caem**,
  **Lamphun** (1969); and ms 4285 (1974). All four are filed as
  [jātaka](/a?s=genre:jataka), and the late dates are notable — this serpent tale
  was still being copied out by hand in Lanna in the 1970s.
- **นาคเขาคำ *Nak khao kham*** — the title reads as "the naga of Khao Kham," the
  golden hill — ms 3243, from
  **Wat Pa O Don Chai** in Mueang district, **Chiang Rai**, dated 1970: 14
  fascicles, 16 folios, complete, with a colophon, in Pali and Thai, in **Thai
  script on khoi paper**. Both of those last facts make it an outlier — it is one
  of only **56 khoi manuscripts** in the whole collection, and one of only **72**
  in plain Thai script against 6,266 in Tham Lanna. *Inference —* a naga tale
  copied late, in the everyday script, on the everyday paper, is a naga tale that
  had left the scriptorium and gone into circulation.
- **นาควิมานสูตร *Nakawimana sut*** — ms 4835, bound with the *Pathainya sut*, at
  [Wat Phra That Lampang Luang](/place/wat-phra-that-lampang-luang/), **Lampang**,
  dated 1787 by the year-name *pi moeng mot*; 15 folios, filed under
  [Buddhist canonical](/a?s=genre:buddhist_canonical) as a *sutta*. This is the
  single non-narrative naga title in the collection — a *vimāna* text, an account
  of a celestial mansion and the deeds that earned it, which is to say a naga
  treated as a destination rather than a character.

*Inference —* subtract the Wat Kittiwong cluster and the catalogue's title-level
naga shrinks to **six**, which sharpens rather than weakens what follows: the
serpent who is genuinely well attested on this shelf is not called naga in a title
at all. He is called Bhūridatta.

## Bhūridatta — the serpent who took the precepts

The old version of this page named "the *Bhūridatta*-type serpent-king tales" and
left it there. The catalogue has them, and they are not a footnote:
**eighteen witnesses carry ภูริทัต / *Phulitat* in the title**, all palm-leaf,
all Tham Lanna, all filed as [jātaka](/a?s=genre:jataka), with dated copies
running **1722 to 1955**. They cluster in **Lampang (7)** and **Chiang Mai (6)** —
three at [Wat Duang Di](/place/wat-duang-di/) alone (1789, 1825, 1844), two at
[Wat Chedi Luang](/place/wat-chedi-luang-chiang-mai/), and single copies as far
out as [Wat Phra That Hariphunchai](/place/wat-phra-that-hariphunchai-woramahawihan/)
in Lamphun. **Bhūridatta is twice as well attested in this archive as every
manuscript that names a naga in its title — and three times better attested than
the ones that securely do.**

*Tradition holds —* the story is the reason. Bhūridatta is a naga prince who
takes the eight precepts and, to keep them, coils himself on an anthill at the
edge of the human world and vows not to move — not to defend himself — for the
whole night, offering his skin, flesh and blood to anyone who wants them. A
snake-charmer takes him at his word: catches him with charms, crushes him in his
hands, breaks his bones, stuffs him into a basket and makes him dance in village
after village for money. He does not resist, because resisting would break the
vow, and the vow is the only thing he came for. The corpus's own devotional
mnemonics compress the whole thing into a phrase — in the Great Assembly
invocation printed in the
[Compendium of Ancient Katha](/m/compendium-katha-from-old-manuals-6966/)
he is listed among the Ten Births as **"Phra Bhuridatta, who observed the precepts
and gave alms."** *Inference —* that is why he out-copies the rest. The Lanna
shelf does not want the naga as a monster; it wants the naga as the being with
the most to lose by being good, and losing it.

## The working naga — nine jobs in the printed treatises

The contributed printed manuals are where the second naga lives, and he is
everywhere in them. He is almost never the topic of a page. He is a shape, a
syllable, a metal, a witness, a candle.

**He is a shape with its own password.** The
[charm-and-amulet treatise](/m/making-sacred-charms-amulets-6983/) (p.14) gives
the inscription formulas keyed to what you are drawing. A banner-shaped yant opens
*ธะชัคคังปะริตตัง มังคะลัง*. A lotus-petal yant opens *ปะทุมะยันตัง สันตัง วิกรึงคะเร*.
And **a naga-shaped yant opens นาคะยันตัง สันตัง วิกรึงคะเร** — the same ground-formula
with the serpent's name slotted in where the shape's name goes. The naga is a
declension of the general [yantra](/a?s=entity:yantra) grammar, not a special case.

**He is a candle you burn down to the end of your bad luck.** The
[Lanna yantra-and-katha manual](/m/yantra-craft-katha-lanna-lineage-6980/)
gives the full procedure for **วิชายันต์เทียนพญานาคสะเดาะเคราะห์**, the
Naga-King Candle-Yantra for Dispelling Misfortune, and the procedure is the
article. First write the person out in full — given name, surname, day, month, BE
year, year of birth, age. Then name the yantra aloud, in the formula, so that the
thing knows what it is: *"this candle-yantra should accordingly be called the
Naga-King Candle-Yantra for Dispelling Misfortune."* Then wake it with a Pali
awakening-verse. Then build it: **take one strand of sacred thread longer than the
person's age**, roll it for a wick, lay a sheet of beeswax in the sun until it is
"moderately softened — judge this by feel," and roll the candle with a head and a
tail. Then light it before the Buddha image and **sit reciting until the candle
burns out.** The wick is longer than the life it is for, and the rite ends when the
wax runs out — the same logic that drives the
[life-extension rite](/a?s=entity:suep_cata), told in wax.

**He is the thing the diagram stands on.** Two pages later the same manual
prints a tower of stacked script-panels crowned with a lotus-bud finial, and
underneath it a naga, **coiled so that his raised head and tail curve up on either
side to form a boat-like base** beneath the whole figure. Not decoration: the
serpent is the hull the yant travels in.

**He is a stencil you can take to a tattooist.** The
[sak-yant tracing book](/m/sak-yant-stencils-tracing-6985/) carries **ยันต์พญานาค**
twice over — a coiled serpent-dragon with a flame-tipped tail and a
spiral amulet-mark at the centre of the coil, ringed by fine cursive incantation
script, then a second variant with the head turned toward a burst of radiating
marks. Earlier in the same book a **five-row นาคราช yant** is printed
directly beside a five-row Ganesha — a rearing scaled serpent flanked by five
vertical columns of script, filed as one entry in a repertoire of powers.

**He is a glyph in the na canon.** The
[Scripture of 108 Magical Na](/m/scripture-108-special-na-6964/) (p.93) runs a
gallery of little machines of loop, zigzag and coil, each with a name: *Na Son
Luk* (hiding the child), *Na Son Chu* (hiding the lover), *Na Chang* (elephant),
*Na Suea* (tiger), *Na Ngu* (snake) — and then, immediately after the snake,
**นะนาคราช, the Naga-King Na**, an open crescent-hook over a capsule frame. The
snake and the serpent-king are adjacent entries and different glyphs; the tradition
files them separately on purpose. (The whole gallery is walkable at
[the 108 Na](/na/).)

**He is a heart-syllable.** The
[Khom-Thai inscription formulas](/m/yantra-inscription-formulas-mixed-khom-thai-6977/)
(p.23) lists the *heart* — the compressed syllable-core — of each power in the
curriculum, and gives **อะงะสะ, "the heart of the Naga King,"** heading a column
that runs on down through the Horse King, the Lord of Death, the Mara King and
Thao Wessuwan to the heart of the wild junglefowl that the
[Kai page](/a?s=entity:kai) follows. Whatever else he is, in
the syllable canon he is a standard entry, learnable like any other.

**He is worn on the body — as an ornament, in the invocation.** The
[Compendium of Ancient Katha](/m/compendium-katha-from-old-manuals-6966/)
carries a teacher-assembling katha that dresses the practitioner in deities, part
by part: the Buddha above the crown and hair, Brahma on the left shoulder,
Narayana on the right, the River Goddess as saliva, the Wind God as the breath of
the mouth — and **the naga prince as a necklace.** The serpent goes where the
serpent goes in every temple carving: around the throat, above the heart.

**He is an alloy.** In the
[Phetcharat great yantra manual](/m/phetcharat-great-yantra-manual-6960/) (p.77) a
takrut instruction lists the metals to inscribe on: **gold, นาค, silver, copper** —
*nak* here being the reddish gold-copper alloy that carries the serpent's name in
Thai metallurgy. The naga is not invoked on that page; he is simply one of the
four things you can beat thin enough to write on. Readers working the
[material lens](/nuea) will recognise the move: a mythic name doing quiet duty as
a technical term.

**And he is leverage in a love-charm.** The
[Akhom of Phra Narai](/m/akhom-magic-king-narai-era-6970/) (p.169) runs a page of
one-line attraction mantras with their delivery instructions, and among them:
**"อิสสะราชา นาคะราชา — this mantra is blown toward a woman so that she comes to
love us."** No explanation, no myth, no apparatus. The serpent-king's name is
simply the strongest thing on the shelf, so it goes in the breath.

## The assembly — where the corpus counts the nagas

Two treatises in this collection stop and take the roll.

The [Mind-Stream Key to Saiyasat](/m/mind-current-key-saiyasat-6984/) (p.129)
is working through the [Mahāsamaya Sutta](/a?s=entity:mahasamai), the great
gathering in the Mahāvana forest, and reaches **"Part 4: Concerning Nagas and
Elephants."** They arrive by watershed: the nagas of the pool named Nabhasa in the
territory of Vesali, with the retinue of **Tacchaka Nagaraja**; Kambala and
Assatara; the nagas of the ford at Payāga with their kinsfolk; the nagas of the
Yamuna; the whole **Dhataraṭṭha** clan with their households. Then the elephant
Erāvaṇa. Then — Part 5 — the garudas, "those divine birds who have clear eyes and
are able to swoop and seize naga kings suddenly." And here the commentary does
something lovely with the fear in the room: *"At that time, fearlessness arose
for all the nagas, because the Buddha had extended his spiritual power, making
them safe from the Garuda king."* The gloss goes on to explain the ordinary state
of affairs — naga-devas live in water, garuda-devas live in trees and on
mountains, they are enemies by nature — *"but when they came together at this
assembly in the Mahavana forest, they all became friends with one another."* An
armistice, in a forest, for the length of a sermon. Elsewhere in the corpus the
war is back on: the same Compendium (p.52) carries **คาถาพญาครุฑ**, the Spell of
the Garuda King, in which the speaker declares himself the garuda-lord arriving
before the Naga King and **commands him aside**.

The [Supreme Maha Mantra Katha](/m/supreme-maha-mantra-katha-6968/)
takes the roll differently — as blessing. Its long litany works through arahants
and nuns and then arrives at the nagas and simply *names them*, four to a verse,
for verse after verse: Sumana, Sumanacala, Aravāḷa, Eḷapattaka, Campeyya,
**Mucalinda**, Kambala; then Kāla, Mahākāla, Saṅkhapāla, Mahodara, Maṇikaṇṭha,
Maṇiakkhi, Nanda and Upananda; then Varuṇa, Dhataraṭṭha, Kuṅkuvila, Apalāla,
Citra, Mahāvīra, Chabyāputta, **Vāsukī**; then Kaṇhāgotama, Aggisikha, Cūḷodara,
Ahicchatta, "the naga kings, with Erāpatha and the rest as their leaders." And
then the ask, which is the best sentence about nagas in the whole archive:
*"Whatever naga kings there are, of fierce venom — that is, venom that spreads
swiftly — of dreadful poison, **having their eyes as weapons**, dwelling in the
water or dwelling on land, dwelling in the mountains, or roaming in the rivers —
may all those naga kings grant us excellent well-being, long life, and freedom
from illness at all times."* Thirty-odd names of the most dangerous beings in the
cosmology, recited in order, so that they will look after you.

*Inference —* this is the naga's real position in the ritual grammar of these
books, and it is why he keeps appearing without ever getting a chapter. He is
neither worshipped nor exorcised. He is **convened.** The
[Vedic katha for chai chatri](/m/warrior-vedic-katha-chai-chatri-6972/) (p.2)
makes it explicit in its deity-convening formula, the one you say before anything
else: *"and the great naga-kings, standing near — the deities dwell in every islet
and rapids of every district"* — invited in the same breath as the
[ruesi](/a?s=entity:lersi) and the sky-devas, with the instruction printed plainly
underneath: *"Before performing any rite whatsoever, one should first recite this
Katha for Convening the Deities."* Every rite in these manuals begins by telling
the serpents in the local water that something is about to happen.

## The naga a monk had to fight

One naga in the corpus is not convened but subdued, and he is famous enough to be
sung weekly. Verse 7 of the **Jaya Maṅgala Gāthā** — the Bāhung, printed in the
[Lanna yantra manual](/m/yantra-craft-katha-lanna-lineage-6980/) — runs:
*"The naga king Nandopananda, learned and of great power, was tamed by the elder
[Moggallāna], his own disciple, appearing as a naga — through the method of
teaching miraculous power the Lord of Sages conquered him."* Moggallāna wins by
becoming what he is fighting.

That verse turns up again as evidence in an argument. In
[Teaching Meditation and Psychic Power](/m/teaching-samadhi-use-mind-power-6981/)
(p.156) the author is defending the study of mind-power against learned monks who
say it is a later accretion, "a low-level branch of knowledge that should not be
studied," and he reaches for the Bāhung: eight displays of *iddhi* in the canon,
seven of them by the Buddha himself, and one delegated —
*"the Buddha taught a stratagem or method for fighting the nāga king to Phra Mahā
Moggallāna, then had Moggallāna display iddhi to fight the nāga king."* Then, having
won the point, he immediately concedes the other half of it: such power exists,
"but it is not good, not proper, and is not something befitting the station of the
Buddha and the Arahants to do." *Inference —* a modern Thai author using a
serpent-fight to argue that serpent-fighting is real and unbecoming, on the same
page, is a fair portrait of how this whole tradition holds its powers.

## The naga on the ordination-day list

The plainest naga in the archive is not sacred at all — it is a checklist. The
[complete ritual handbook](/m/conducting-religious-rituals-complete-6971/), a
modern printed manual of ceremonies, sets out the Rite of Going Forth and Higher
Ordination, and it uses **นาค** flatly, as the ordinary word for the man being
ordained. The items to prepare (p.18): **ผ้านุ่งนาค**, cloth for the naga to wear;
a raised tray for the triple robe; a ceremonial fan; flowers to drape over the
robes; one-baht coins to scatter as alms; and "three lotus flowers, three incense
sticks and two candles — **for the naga**." Then the order of events, which is the
naga's last hour: **โกนผมนาค**, shave the naga's head; **แห่นาครอบอุโบสถ**, carry
the naga in procession around the ordination hall; pay respect to the boundary
stones; **นาคเข้าสู่อุโบสถ**, the naga enters. Inside, he kneels, bows three times,
turns to face his parents, bows again, and takes the triple robe from their hands;
he walks on his knees to the preceptor; he goes out behind the hall to change; and
when he comes back in the robes, the manual has stopped calling him a naga and
started calling him a novice. Where two men ordain together the manual has a word
for that too — the **นาคคู่**, the paired nagas (p.32).

The example this handbook prints for the group rite is
*"a group ordination of naga among Royal Thai Air Force officials,"* with a
chaplain requesting the precepts and a presiding officer working down the order of
service. *Inference —* nothing in this section is mythological, and that is the
point. The Vinaya's rejected serpent has ended up as a piece of Thai
administrative vocabulary, on a printed schedule, in an air force ceremony. It is
the single most thoroughly domesticated naga in the collection.

## The living market

The serpent is also the fastest-moving folk figure in the archive's
[market layer](/w/products). Of the 13,020 amulet listings in the Lazada
snapshot, **79 name พญานาค, นาคราช or พระยานาค in the title** — brass takrut, coiled
figures, rings, *ลูกแก้วพญานาค* naga crystal-orbs, and **บ่วงนาคบาศ**, the
naga-noose. Prices sit low and fast: **median ฿85**, from ฿7 to ฿3,190. The
Mekong household is present and named — **5 listings** invoke **คำชะโนด**, the
Kham Chanot grove, and **4** name **ปู่ศรีสุทโธ**, its naga lord. The effect
vocabulary is trade and flow: wealth-drawing, *เรียกทรัพย์*, "money pouring in."
*Inference —* this is the naga register the palm-leaf catalogue is silent about
and the printed manuals only glance at, running at full volume in the present
tense, and it is Isan-inflected rather than Lanna — the Mekong's serpent, sold
nationally. A crawl that treated Phaya Nak as a Lanna subject would miss where his
current centre of gravity actually is.

## Notes

- **The count to remember is nine, six, and eighteen.** Nine manuscripts put นาค
  in the title; probably only six are about a naga; and **eighteen** more, on the
  [jātaka shelf](/browse?genre=jataka), name Bhūridatta, who is one. The old
  version of this page reported eight and did not count Bhūridatta at all. Any
  future naga tally should start from the birth-tale hero, not the word.
- **Wat Kittiwong's cluster needs a reader — and this is the correction.** The
  three *Mahanak watthu* witnesses (ms 1270, 1271, 1283) carry older PLMP titles
  that read two of them as an *elder's* life, มหานาคเถรวัตถุ and มหาพกุลเถรวัตถุ,
  not a serpent's. Nine folios of transcription resolves the archive's biggest
  naga cluster one way or the other. It is the cheapest high-value job on this
  page.
- **The functional naga is a section-level phenomenon.** Everything in "The
  working naga" above came from *page content*, not titles, and all of it from the
  contributed printed shelf. *Inference —* the same material is near-certainly
  sitting inside untitled ยันต์-and-คาถา bundles from
  [Nan, Phrae and Lampang](/atlas), invisible to a title search. The search image
  is now specific: look for the opening **นาคะยันตัง สันตัง วิกรึงคะเร**, for the
  heart-syllable **อะงะสะ**, for a candle rite measured against a person's age, and
  for a coiled base under a yant-tower.
- **Where else he is standing.** Read this page beside
  [Mahāsamaya](/a?s=entity:mahasamai) for the assembly, [yantra](/a?s=entity:yantra)
  and [katha](/a?s=entity:katha) for the forms he is filed under, and
  [Vessantara](/a?s=entity:vessantara) — the two of them share the same devotional
  furniture — the great invocation at
  the [Compendium](/m/compendium-katha-from-old-manuals-6966/) runs through the
  Ten Births and names Bhūridatta and Vessantara a few names apart, on one breath.
