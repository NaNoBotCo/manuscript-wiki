## What this is

**Phanya In** (พระยาอิน, in Lanna spelling often พยาอิน) is Indra — the old Vedic
king of the gods, taken into Buddhism as **Sakka** (Pali), lord of the
Tāvatiṃsa heaven on the summit of Mount Meru. Standard Thai writes him
พระอินทร์ (*Phra In*); the northern leaves drop the silent ทร and write อิน, as
it is said. Of the eighteen titles below, only one spells him the Bangkok way.

He is the god who comes down. In the Buddhist story he is a devotee, not a
rival: he questions the Buddha, he tests Vessantara, he appears when the
virtuous are in trouble. In Chiang Mai he is also the giver of the city
pillar. And far from Lanna, in China, his name was hung on one of the most
quoted pictures in Buddhist thought — a net with a jewel at every knot. This
page follows him through the leaves this archive holds, then out to the net.

## Eighteen witnesses, six provinces

The catalogue holds **18 manuscripts** whose titles name him, from Chiang
Mai, Phrae, Chiang Rai, Lampang, Lamphun and Nan. Ten carry a date; they
run from **CS 1124 (1762 CE) to CS 1309 (1947 CE)**. They fall into four
kinds.

**The questions of Sakka — five witnesses.** The *Sakkapañha Sutta* (Dīgha
Nikāya 21) is the canonical text in which Sakka comes to the Buddha with
questions about envy, craving and peace, and leaves a stream-enterer. The
oldest dated Indra witness in the whole catalogue is a copy of it:
[Sakkapanha](/m/sakkapanha-2-4486/), 44 pages, CS 1124 (1762), at Wat Chedi
Luang in Chiang Mai. Wat Sung Men in Phrae — the great library temple — holds
four more: a twelve-fascicle set copied twice, in
[CS 1192 (1830)](/m/sakkapanha-phuk-sip-song-927/) and
[CS 1204 (1842)](/m/sakkapanha-phuk-sip-song-2-953/), an undated
[Sakkapanha](/m/sakkapanha-2408/), and a
[Sakkapanha cadok](/m/sakkapanha-cadok-2370/) that frames it as a jataka.

**The tamnan of Phanya In — ten witnesses.** *Tamnan* are legends and
chronicles. *Tamnan Phanya In*, sometimes with *cao fa* (lord of the sky)
added, turns up at
[Wat Chiang Man](/m/tamnan-phanya-cao-fa-786/) (CS 1234, 1872), at Wat Sung
Men ([2029](/m/tamnan-phanya-2029/)), at Wat Chedi Luang
([4512](/m/nangsue-tamnan-phanya-4512/)), at Wat Lai Hin Luang in Lampang
([4710](/m/tamnan-phanya-2-4710/)), and at Wat Klang in Phrae
([CS 1309, 1947](/m/tamnan-phanya-cao-fa-2-3466/)). At Wat Phra That Chang Kham
in Nan it is bound into a volume of
[old law](/m/kotmai-bolan-tamnan-phanya-244/) (CS 1269, 1907). Four more carry
longer titles: *Phanya Inthathirat* — Indra the overlord — at
[Wat Pa Bong, Chiang Rai](/m/tamnan-phanya-intathilat-cao-fa-6299/) (1922) and
in [a 1942 copy](/m/tamnan-phanya-intathilat-3489/); *Phanya In son
lokkathat* — Phanya In teaching the elements of the world — at
[Wat Mahawan, Lamphun](/m/tamnan-phanya-son-lokkathat-3933/) (1910); and
*Phanya Inta lokkahini* at
[Wat Phra That Hariphunchai](/m/tamnan-phanya-inta-lokkahini-3472/) (1935).

**His teachings — two witnesses.** *Owat kham son haeng Phanya In*, the
admonitions of Phanya In, at [Wat Chiang Man](/m/owat-kam-son-haeng-phanya-762/)
and [Wat Sung Men](/m/owat-kam-son-haeng-phanya-2-2557/).

**His sermon — one witness.** *Tesana Phanya In long ma*, the sermon of
Phanya In come down, at
[Wat Huai Khrai Luang, Chiang Rai](/m/tesana-phanya-long-ma-cai-cat-3420/).

*Inference —* the catalogue sorts thirteen of the eighteen as didactic or
chronicle rather than canonical. Outside the one sutta, Lanna kept Phanya In
mostly as a teacher and a figure of story: the god who descends to instruct.
The titles alone say this much; what each leaf tells is for the reader who
opens it.

## The pillar

*Tradition —* before Chiang Mai, the Lua people of the valley prayed to Indra,
and he sent down a pillar to keep the land — the **Sao Inthakhin**
(เสาอินทขิล), *khila* being a stake or post. It stood at the city's navel, at
what is now [Wat Inthakhin Sadue Mueang](/place/wat-inthakhin-sadue-muang/),
until King Kawila moved it in 1800 to
[Wat Chedi Luang](/place/wat-chedi-luang-chiang-mai/), where the city still
honours it every year at the start of the rains. The same temple holds the
oldest dated Indra manuscript in this archive, the 1762 Sakkapañha. The
city's blessing that honours the pillar is the rite described under
[the four corners](/a?s=entity:jaeng) and [suep chata](/a?s=entity:suep_cata);
the city's founding is under [Mangrai](/a?s=entity:mangrai).

He is also on the leaf in the most-copied story in Lanna: in the
[Vessantara](/a?s=entity:vessantara) the *Sakkaban* chapter is his, the god
who comes disguised as a brahmin and asks the prince for his wife.

## The net

The picture most of the world now attaches to his name did not come from
Lanna. In the Chinese Huayan school, working from the *Avataṃsaka* (Flower
Garland) Sutra, the monk Dushun (557–640) described a net hung over Indra's
palace, running on in every direction, with a jewel at each knot — each jewel
reflecting every other jewel, and each reflection holding all the rest. Two
generations on, Fazang built it for Empress Wu out of mirrors: ten of them around a
lamp and an image of the Buddha. The point was **dependent origination** —
ปฏิจจสมุปบาท (*patitcha-samupbat*), the teaching that nothing arises on its
own — drawn as a single image.

None of the eighteen Lanna titles names the net, and the Huayan texts are
not in this catalogue. *Inference —* the net is Chinese Mahayana and the
Lanna leaves are Theravada; the god crossed between them, the picture stayed
where it was made. The teaching under it is common to both, and the
Sakkapañha itself is a sermon on how craving and envy arise from other
things.

The net has since been read a dozen other ways — as Hindu *māyā* (in
Sanskrit, *indrajāla*, Indra's net, came to mean a conjuring trick), as
Thich Nhat Hanh's *interbeing*, as Leibniz's living mirrors, as the
mathematics of round mirrors reflecting each other. A companion site draws
all of it, with the mirror arithmetic running live:
[Indra's Net, Drawn](https://nanobotco.github.io/indras-net/). Its
[readings page](https://nanobotco.github.io/indras-net/readings/) gives
sixteen interpretations side by side, the Thai one among them; its
[mirror room](https://nanobotco.github.io/indras-net/story/) rebuilds
Fazang's demonstration.
