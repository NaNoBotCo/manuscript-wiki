## What this is

**Jātaka** (Lanna *chadok*) are the birth-stories: tales of the Buddha's previous
lives as the Bodhisatta, working his way through the perfections across some five
hundred existences. In the Pali canon they sit in the *Khuddaka-nikāya*, grouped
by the number of verses each tale carries — the *nipāta*, which surfaces directly
in the titles here (*Wisati nibat*, the twenty-verse group; *Panca nibat*; *Sap
tingsa nibat*). But the Lanna jātaka corpus is wider than the canon. Its living
heart is the **paññāsa jātaka**, the extra-canonical "fifty births" composed in
mainland Southeast Asia, and above all the **Mahāchat** — the Vessantara, the
Great Birth, whose annual recitation (*Thet Mahachat*) is one of the central
preaching festivals of the northern year.

So while this is not a *wichaa* genre, it is not inert scripture either. These were
the preacher's working repertoire, copied to be read aloud; the local verse retellings
that fill out the collection (*Along* this and that) are Lanna literature as much as
they are Buddhist canon.

## The shape of the collection

Jātaka is one of the largest genres in the collection — over 1,600 witnesses — and, like the
canonical texts it sits beside, it is written almost entirely in **Tham Lanna** (94%)
on **palm-leaf** (88%). Paper barely registers here: a jātaka is a durable sermon-text,
copied into the same canonical format as the scripture it accompanies, not a working
almanac to be opened flat.

On paper the collection looks like a Phrae–Lampang tradition (Phrae 27%, Lampang 22%),
but that shape is deceptive and worth reading carefully — see the first note below.

## Notes

- **The place-distribution is a library artifact, not a map of production.** A single
  repository, **Wat Sung Men** in Phrae, supplies more than a fifth of the entire genre
  from one temple (over 350 witnesses). Phrae leads because Wat Sung Men's
  library survived and was catalogued, not because Phrae "wrote more jātaka." Read every
  province and temple count in this genre against that fact.
- **Canon and apocrypha are collapsed under one label.** The *nipāta* groupings of the
  canonical five hundred, the extra-canonical *paññāsa jātaka*, and the Mahāchat all
  arrive here as plain "Jataka" — including a batch recently reclaimed from a mislabelled
  "Buddhist Tale." Splitting these apart — canonical *nipāta* vs. *paññāsa* vs.
  Vessantara — is the obvious next taxonomy pass, and the one most useful to a reader.
- **Overlap with recitation.** Because the Mahāchat is performed, not just read, expect
  jātaka to shade into the [liturgy and chanting](/a?s=genre:liturgy_chanting) corpus
  wherever a title names a *thet* (sermon) or a festival recitation.
