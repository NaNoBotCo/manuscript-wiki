## What this is

**Saiyasat** (ไสยศาสตร์) is the science of making something happen — the
efficacious half of northern religion. Its instruments are the
[nyan](/a?s=entity:yantra) (the drawn diagram), the
[katha](/a?s=entity:katha) (the spoken formula), and the consecration —
*pluk sek*, *phutthaphisek* — that turns an inert object or an ordinary morning
into something that works. It is the esoteric core this archive exists to
document, and every one of its **276 witnesses** is flagged research-priority.

This is also the page where the archive's two halves meet and refuse to
reconcile politely. On one side sits the Lanna palm-leaf and folding-paper
shelf, whose titles are overwhelmingly benedictory: rice-offerings,
robe-consecrations, life-extensions, soul-callings. On the other sit fourteen
contributed printed treatises whose pages are transcribed and translated in
full — and those pages will tell you which day to forge a knife out of iron
robbed from seven cremation grounds. Both are saiyasat. The rest of this page
is about how that is possible.

## The story, first — a boy, a mantra, and a gun that burst

The best account of how this knowledge actually moves between people is told in
the first person, by a man remembering himself at eight years old, in
[Mind Stream — Key to Saiyasat](/m/mind-current-key-saiyasat-6984/) (p.36–38).

Three of his father's students walk in from Ban On Nuea, in Pak Phanang
district, each carrying an air rifle. The narrator explains why, flatly: tigers
were fierce there and bandits plentiful, so every house kept a gun and you
carried it wherever you went. The three men hunt together, so naturally they
come to study together. They have already passed through the invulnerability
branch; this time they want ***maha-ut*** — the charm that stops a gun from
firing. The father teaches it, and attaches one prohibition, which he states
twice: *never cover a gun barrel with your hand* — not your own, not a friend's
— because if you hold your breath and recite over a covered muzzle, the gun will
not fire out of the muzzle. It will burst instead.

The boy is asleep in the same room. At dawn the three men go out to wash their
faces, and he wakes up alone with their rifles propped against the wall. So he
does what any eight-year-old would do with a brand-new piece of forbidden
knowledge. He holds his breath, presses his palm over the muzzle of Mr Saeng's
gun, and recites — *na ut mo ut na mo phutthaya dtantani* — for several held
breaths. The men come back in, collect their guns, and go home.

Three months later Mr Saeng returns with a new gun and a grievance. Five days
after getting home he had chased a wounded deer, fired, and the barrel had
split open in the middle. He is certain Mr Nuan did it, and he says out loud
what a man says out loud in that world: had it been anyone but a fellow
disciple of the same teacher, he would have had to shoot him dead. Mr Nuan
comes and denies it. Then the father turns to his son.

The interrogation that follows is the whole ethical structure of the tradition
compressed into six questions. *Have you memorised the maha-ut mantra?* — I
have. *Recite it.* — he recites it. *How is it done?* — hold the breath and
chant with the palm on the navel, every day before sleeping. *What is
forbidden?* — to press the hand on the muzzle of your own gun or anyone else's.
*Did you do it to Saeng's gun?* — once. And then the verdict, which is not
about the gun: *do you know that doing such a thing is called breaking an
instruction? From now on, never break an instruction again.*

The writer's own moral is stranger than the story. He tells it, he says, to
show that even the most foolish person — a child who could not yet write a
single letter and knew only the *namo* — got a result, because he had no doubt
whatsoever. The middling student, half-learned, fails; the treatise's word for
what ruins him is *vicikicchā*, canonical Buddhist doubt (p.36). *Inference —*
the tradition's own theory of failure is not that the formula is weak. It is
that the practitioner is divided. That is why so much of what follows is
discipline rather than recipe.

## What the shelf actually tells you to do

The treatises are not shy. [Making Sacred Charms and Amulets](/m/making-sacred-charms-amulets-6983/)
announces its own scale on p.22: **42 kinds of amulet-making manual and 158
different forms**, indexed like a parts catalogue — Nang Kwak, Hanuman, the
tiger-fang, the *wua thanu* bull-of-the-bow, the *palad khik*, the
stillborn-child *luk krok*, the ritual knife, the Kuman Thong, boiled
love-charm oil, boiled magic lip-wax, a rattan rod for subduing spirits, a
figure of a [lersi](/a?s=entity:lersi) to be moulded.

Three entries are worth reading as procedure, because procedure is where the
audacity lives.

**The ritual knife** (*mid mo*, p.86–88) begins as metallurgy with a grudge.
The smith is to gather ten irons and forge them into one blade: iron from a
stupa finial, from a prang tower, from a *prasat* spire, *kamchat* and *kamchai*
iron, "good" iron, Nam Phi iron, shrimp-tail iron, palace-gate iron — and iron
from seven cremation grounds. While he works he keeps the five or eight
precepts; the forging is done on a Saturday and a Tuesday, with vegetarian
offerings and *bai si* trays set to left and right and monks chanting the
*karaniya* seven times. Finished, the blade is quenched three times and seven
times in prai-oil. The handle is then loaded with powder ground from the
foreheads of four bad deaths — one who died in childbirth, one drowned, one
hanged, one struck by lightning — mixed with flower-tips and medicinal powder,
each katha recited over it 108 times. The claimed effects escalate calmly:
scratch a line on the ground and the enemy stops there; plant the knife at a
crevice and everyone sleeps; even an invulnerable man can be cut by it. And
then, quietly, the condition on the whole apparatus: *hold to truthfulness and
it is efficacious. The knife succeeds by force of mind.* The second method
(p.88) supplies the awakening chant, a rolling cosmological boast that names
every foreign iron it intends to command — Indian, Hollander, English, Khom,
Cham-China, Brahmin-Lawa, Burma, Mon-Mataban, Lao and Karen — a Southeast Asian
arms inventory rendered as liturgy.

**The Kuman Thong** (p.91, p.94–95) is the passage the market never stopped
citing, and it is smaller and sadder than its reputation. The practitioner is
directed to a child who died *tai thang klom*, whole — mother and infant
together — or one who died while the mother was still in post-partum
confinement on the *kradan fai*, the fire-board; best on a Saturday or a
Tuesday. You go to fetch it with a candle at the head and a candle at the feet,
two *krathong* trays of spicy shrimp and fish salad, two bananas. You explain
yourself to the child: *father will take you to raise as his son.* You tell it
where the house is, so it can find its way. And then the text remembers one
more party and turns startlingly courteous — inform the ***mae sü***, the
spirit-mother who took the child to raise as her own, "because we are going to
ask for it" (p.95). A negotiation with the dead conducted according to the
etiquette of adoption. The same page's practical register is the household's:
feed it rice daily, wake it before it eats, bind it closed with a katha before
you put it away. Read this beside [Khun Phaen](/a?s=entity:khun_phaen), whose
epic supplies the practice's origin story, and beside the archive's own
[Hun Payont](/hun) — the animated servant-figure is the same idea with the
grief taken out.

**The tiger-fang** (p.77–78) starts as carving — a crouching tiger, a seated
tiger, a leaping tiger, cut from a real fang, steeped in scented oil — and then
opens into the single strangest page in the corpus. The awakening chant becomes
a roll-call, and what it calls is a company of named women: Mae-nang Et,
Mae-nang Sai, Mae-nang Chop, Mae-nang Phueak, Mae-nang Hongsa *who veils her
husband's eyes*, Mae-nang Saem *who steeps a hundred pots of medicine to make
the light of a beloved man*. Thirty deities are summoned to hold the reciter's
arms and lead the van, forty to hold his arms and guard the rear, "according to
the day's strength." Then the register drops from cosmic to intimate without a
seam: come and stay at the belly, come and stay in the lap; cling to the shin
and do not slip. *Inference —* the fang is nominally a warrior's charm, but its
consecration is a love-charm wearing armour, and the seam between the two is
invisible because in this tradition there is no seam — *kong kraphan* and
*saneh* run on one engine.

## The rules — a discipline before it is a repertoire

Every treatise that gives a method also gives the fence around it, and the
fences are more revealing than the methods.

- **Three prohibitions on the invulnerability craft**
  ([Teaching Meditation and Psychic Power](/m/teaching-samadhi-use-mind-power-6981/),
  p.27). One: do not pass the formula on — five full years of study, or a
  teacher-raising ceremony first. Two: do not use paper bearing Thai or Khom
  script to wipe yourself. Three: do not curse someone's mother in a fight. A
  transmission rule, a script-reverence rule, and a temper rule, ranked
  together as equals.
- **A weekday diet.** [The Life of Luang Por Thob](/m/life-luang-por-thob-katha-he-used-6974/)
  (p.50) forbids a food to each birth-weekday: water spinach for those born on
  Sunday, coriander for Monday, pickled cleome for Tuesday, boiled aubergine
  for Wednesday, pork for Thursday, *phak bang* for Friday, moringa leaves for
  Saturday. Break it and the charm will not take; keep it and your amulets will
  outperform other people's. *Inference —* this is [horā](/a?s=genre:astrology)
  operating inside saiyasat: the birth-day that sets your fate in the
  astrologer's table also sets what you may eat, and efficacy is made to depend
  on the calendar the diviner keeps.
- **The blind yantra.** [Yantra Inscription Formulas in Mixed Khom and Thai](/m/yantra-inscription-formulas-mixed-khom-thai-6977/)
  (p.42) sets the drafting rule and the failure state in one breath: the line
  must be drawn in a single continuous stroke, finishing exactly as the formula
  finishes, because stopping partway is *spoiled*. And the one absolute
  prohibition — no character or numeral may ever cross a line of the diagram.
  Such a yantra is called a **blind yantra** and cannot be used. The same page
  explains why the script is Khom, and why the yantra compartments are so often
  filled with bare numerals: a number stands in for a formula too long to
  write, so the numeral **7** in a cell means the heart-formula of the seven
  books of the Abhidhamma. The [108 Na](/na/) are the same compression, one
  glyph further down.
- **What it costs.** [Yantra Craft and Katha of the Lanna Lineage](/m/yantra-craft-katha-lanna-lineage-6980/)
  (p.71) prices a working. White flowers, incense, candles, scented water,
  perfumed water, a bottle of Mekhong rum and a bottle of foreign liquor, all
  into one basin — and a **minimum teacher's fee of 998 baht**. Best performed
  at a cremation pyre; next best a graveyard; at home if you must. Three times
  in full. Then three prohibitions again, one of them the drying-rail rule that
  also appears in the corpus's oldest conduct list.
- **The conduct list itself.** [Compendium of Katha from Old Manuals](/m/compendium-katha-from-old-manuals-6966/)
  (p.15–16) does something unexpected: rather than invent house rules, it
  reprints *Sawatdirak*, the didactic verse attributed to Sunthorn Phu, as the
  standing daily observance for anyone studying spells. Face east at sunrise
  and east again when eating; north brings ill fortune and loss of life. Cut
  your nails on Wednesday and Monday. Bathe facing downstream, never up.
  Never step over amulets, tattooed cords, or weapons. Do not pass beneath a
  bridge, a house beam, or a fence rail, "so that the power of one's spells
  will remain potent." And a wardrobe keyed to the week — creamy white on
  Monday, purple with indigo on Tuesday, orange on Wednesday, green on
  Thursday, storm-cloud grey on Friday for going to war, black on Saturday,
  best of all.

## Demonstrations, and a man who took a sword to another man's arm

Two treatises put the claims where they can be checked, and both are more
uncomfortable than a sceptic expects.

[Akhom Magic of Phra Narai](/m/akhom-magic-king-narai-era-6970/) opens by
sorting the whole field into two categories — the kind that helps its user, and
the kind that helps its user by wrecking someone else (p.15). Its account of
the second category is unsparing: burying a carved effigy of an enemy near his
house; ***plòi khun***, enchanting a strip of flesh or hide until it can walk
and releasing it into the air to enter whoever it meets, fatal if it cannot be
countered; twisting the entrails by binding. "We who are Buddhists should never
engage in using mantras wrongly in this way" (p.18). Then, having condemned the
misuse, the author testifies for the defence — and the testimony is the passage
that will not leave you. A man who had finished his chant held out his arm to
be struck. The author picked up a very sharp sword and struck down at the base
of the arm with full force, and admits in the same sentence that he did not
stop to think what would follow if he took the man's arm off. There was a mark
like a thorn-scratch and blood oozing drop by drop. Even so, he closes, he
still cannot say for certain whether the man was truly invulnerable or whether
the blade failed for some other reason (p.17). *Inference —* the archive's own
sceptical apparatus is inside the tradition, not outside it.

[Training the Power of the Mind, vol. 2](/m/training-power-mind-vol-2-6979/)
does the same in public and keeps a diary of it (p.164–166). The author lists
his demonstrations by date and venue: the Signal Corps Regiment at Bang Sue in
December 1954; the Psychiatric Association and then the Buddhist Association at
Wat Bowon in March 1956; **television, Channel 4, on 15 April 1956**; the
Astrologers' Association of Thailand that September; the *Phim Thai* newspaper
at his own house in 1957; Channel 7 in 1960, that time taking mercury in
through the palm and calling it back out. The standard programme is three
feats — gripping a chain heated red-hot, plunging a hand into boiling coconut
oil and scooping some out to eat, and Mr Yu jumping from a table onto broken
glass and rolling on it — with the audience invited to try. Then the Cold War
walks in. A delegation of five foreign representatives from a SEATO meeting,
September 1960; an American Air Force engineering officer from Don Mueang who
asked to test invulnerability at the house; and on 28 September 1962, the Army
Education Department's hall, with the head of JUSMAG present and an American
counter-guerrilla training team — where **Lieutenant Colonel Brown, head of the
American training team, put his own hand into the boiling oil and was also
stabbed in the arm**. The same volume elsewhere reframes the whole craft as
medicine: mind-power is for treating illness, "such as treating wounds by
releasing the power of invulnerability" (p.97). And
[Teaching Meditation and Psychic Power](/m/teaching-samadhi-use-mind-power-6981/)
teaches the technique as a curriculum with a testing protocol — consecrated
lime paste touched to the side of the neck on a held breath (p.20), the test
scheduled at least a day apart from the consecration so no doubt muddies the
result (p.25), a variant for making war elephants and horses invulnerable by
smearing the lime on the animal's neck (p.28) — and insists throughout that the
operator first make the mind loving toward every person alive (p.23).

## What the Lanna shelf holds

Now turn to the palm-leaf and folding-paper catalogue, and the register changes
completely.

Ritual is emphatically a **Nan** genre. Of the 276 witnesses, **115 are from
Nan** — more than the next three muang combined (Phayao 36, Lampang 29, Phrae
26), with Chiang Mai at 18 and Chiang Rai at 11. Three Nan temples carry the
weight, and the archive's own [findings](/findings) already flag them as the
most magic-dense shelves in the collection: **Wat Si Mongkhon (Kong)** and
**Wat Pa Mueat** with 30 apiece and **Wat Hia** with 23. Almost all of it is
**Tham Lanna** (259 of 276) in the mixed Pali-and-Lan Na register. Its physical
support splits nearly evenly — 140 mulberry-paper *phap sa* against 117
palm-leaf — where [astrology](/a?s=genre:astrology) leans hard toward paper.
*Inference —* the consecration and offering texts were copied into the durable
canonical format alongside the liturgy they accompany, while the working
almanac was not; the split is a fossil of two different kinds of use.

**109 of the 276 have no title at all**, and 44 of those are catalogued only by
what the cataloguer could see inside — *nyan* and/or *katha* — a bundle of
diagrams and formulae with no name on the cover. This is the same lesson the
[paṭiloma page](/a?s=entity:patiloma) teaches: this register lives at *section*
level, below the resolution of a title search. Across the genre, 55 titles name a katha, 29 name a nyan, and
**16 name both in one breath** — *nyan lae khatha*, over and over, the drawn
and the spoken filed as one object.

The titles that *do* speak describe a working ritualist's year, and they are
concrete to the point of bluntness:

- **Consecration.** ***Putthaphisek*** (พุทธาภิเสก), the rite that makes an
  image a Buddha, is the genre's deepest root — 10 witnesses here and 15 across
  the corpus, and the earliest dated manuscript in this genre by well over a
  century: **ms 4580, dated 1622**, at
  [Wat Lai Hin Luang](/place/wat-lai-hin-luang/) in Lampang — the next-oldest
  dated witness in the genre is 1766. Alongside it sits
  ms 522 from Wat Pa Mueat, whose contents include ***คำไขตาพระเจ้า*** —
  *the words for opening the Lord's eyes*.
- **Un-doing.** 17 titles name a ***thon*** and four name ***khuet***, the
  Lanna taboo-pollution: ms 220 at Wat Phra That Chang Kham binds together the
  withdrawal of a death-spirit, of all *khuet*, of disease, and of everyone who
  died a *tai hong* death; ms 490 at Wat Pa Mueat includes a recitation to
  un-do a charnel ground. The [thon](/a?s=entity:sut_thon) page follows this
  thread.
- **Calling and extending.** 32 titles name a ***khwan*** and 12 a
  ***suep cata***, often together — ms 423 at Wat Hia pairs a grandmother's
  soul-calling with a life-extension. The rice gets both (ms 328, ms 333,
  ms 450); so does the buffalo (ms 319, ms 530), and so does an elephant
  (ms 538 at Wat Pa Mueat, bound with elephant-physiognomy, a Tai Yuan primer,
  and *holasat*). See [su khwan](/a?s=entity:su_khwan) and
  [suep cata](/a?s=entity:suep_cata), and the archive's own living
  [su khwan for machines](/sukhwan).
- **Brushing and sending.** ms 322 gives a method for *brushing away
  forest-spirits*; ms 323 for brushing cattle and brushing danger; ms 545 for
  sending off the misfortune of a house fire; ms 511 includes the binding of an
  infant's wrists.
- **Feeding and giving.** Rice-offering verses run right through — *puca khao
  pha mueang kaeo*, *kham thwai khao mathupayat*, the 49 rice-balls (ms 86,
  ms 372) — plus eight *wen tan* dedications and the *ongkalakkhana* of a
  *mahakathin* robe (ms 45, Wat Phra Sing, 1849).

*Inference —* nothing on the Lanna shelf is a printed treatise's catalogue of
charms, and nothing in the printed treatises is a village's offering calendar;
but the same technology runs both. The formula is recited over the rice and
over the knife alike. The catalogue's raw label for 262 of these — a single
undifferentiated *Custom / Ritual* — is what happens when a metadata scheme
meets a science it has no column for.

## Handling this material

This archive treats esoteric material descriptively. The catalogue and these
articles record the *existence, provenance and shape* of a rite; where a
contributed treatise is already a published book, we summarise what its page
says and cite the page so a reader can check us. We do not present this as an
instruction manual, we do not reproduce untranslated activation formulae as
though handing them over, and material a lineage would treat as *khong khru*
(restricted teacher-knowledge) is described only at the level a public
finding-aid needs. The treatises themselves are stricter than we are, and say
so on the page — see the transmission rule at
[Teaching Meditation and Psychic Power](/m/teaching-samadhi-use-mind-power-6981/)
p.27. Takedown and correction requests go to 530kings@proton.me.

## Notes

- **The bucket is coarse.** 262 of the 276 arrived under one raw label,
  *Custom / Ritual*, so real distinctions — consecration, offering, protective,
  un-doing, life-extension, soul-calling — are collapsed. Splitting this into
  sub-genres is the obvious next taxonomy pass, and the title evidence for the
  split is already sitting in the counts above.
- **Where to go next.** [Browse the genre](/browse?genre=magic_ritual) to see
  the 276 in provenance order; [find by need](/need) approaches the same
  material from the other end, by what a person came wanting; [what it is made
  of](/nuea) tracks the material axis — prai, bone, metal, herb — that the
  amulet manuals above spend most of their pages on; and the
  [glossary](/glossary/) holds the emic vocabulary these titles use.
- **Read it with its neighbours.** [Astrology](/a?s=genre:astrology) supplies
  the day, the hour and the direction that nearly every rite here depends on;
  [liturgy and chanting](/a?s=genre:liturgy_chanting) is the same technology
  performed in public, in the vihara, by monks. Where a title names both a
  *holasat* and a *katha*, the object is a household manual and the genre line
  is only recording which half won.
- **A crawl and transcription priority.** The 44 untitled *nyan*-and-*katha*
  bundles are the highest-value unread material in the collection. The printed
  treatises give the search image: look for the single-stroke inscription rule,
  the weekday food prohibition, the 108-times consecration count, and the
  numeral standing in for a heart-formula. Anything that surfaces a named na or
  a named yant under a Nan or Lampang hand is a finding.
