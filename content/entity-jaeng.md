## What this is

**แจ่ง** (*chaeng*) is the Lanna word for **corner** — standard Thai says มุม
(*mum*) — and in Chiang Mai the word survives chiefly in four proper names:
the four bastions where the old city's moat turns its right angles. Going
clockwise from the northeast: **แจ่งศรีภูมิ** (Chaeng Si Phum, in the older
pronunciation สะหลีภูมิ *sali phum* — ศรี glory, Skt. *śrī* + ภูมิ ground,
Skt. *bhūmi*), **แจ่งก๊ะต๊ำ** (Chaeng Katam — a *katam* is a bamboo fish
trap), **แจ่งกู่เฮือง** (Chaeng Ku Hueang — กู่ *ku*, a reliquary stupa +
เฮือง *hueang*, of which more below), and **แจ่งหัวลิน** (Chaeng Hua Lin —
หัว head + ลิน *lin*, a water conduit in the northern tongue). The names are
a map of their own: water enters the moat at the head-of-the-conduit corner,
the highest ground, and leaves past the fish trap at the lowest.

They are not ruins with plaques. Every June the city's annual blessing
(ทำบุญเมือง — สืบชะตาเมือง, the [suep chata](/a?s=entity:suep_cata) rite
performed for a whole city) is held **simultaneously at ten stations: the
five gates, the four แจ่ง, and the navel**. The corners still work.

## The four, by name

**ศรีภูมิ — northeast.** The auspicious ground. *Tradition —* the chronicle
tradition begins the city's construction at this corner, and the taksa
scheme (below) places ศรี, glory, in its sector. When the mediumship rites
were separated from the court-run [Inthakhin](/place/wat-chedi-luang-chiang-mai/)
festival around 1900, it was to Chaeng Si Phum that the medium elements were
relegated (Tanabe 2000:308) — the corner as the keeping-place for what the
center set down.

**ก๊ะต๊ำ — southeast.** The lowest corner, where the moat's water finds its
way out; the fish trap is the etymology the sources agree on, and it is a
working image — where water leaves, fish gather, and a trap goes. A shrine
stands at this corner today: the
[ศาลเจ้าพ่อหลักเมืองแจ่งกระต๊ำ](/place/chao-pho-chaeng-katam-shrine/), a
city-pillar-lord shrine keeping the corner in the present tense. (Note the
shrine's own sign spells it กระต๊ำ against the more common ก๊ะต๊ำ — the word
is Lanna and the spellings float.)

**กู่เฮือง — southwest.** The corner in the กาลกิณี (kalakini, misfortune)
sector of the taksa wheel, in the quarter through which the dead
traditionally leave the city. The name-story in circulation — that the กู่
holds or honours a jailer, **หมื่นเฮือง** (Mün Hueang, หมื่น being the rank)
— is graded **REPORTED** in this wiki's citation basis: one secondary
source, no primary attestation found yet. *Inference —* a plainer reading
also exists: เฮือง is the northern cognate of เรือง, glowing, which would
make the name simply "the shining reliquary." Which reading the leaf
supports is an open question below.

**หัวลิน — northwest.** The head of the ลิน, the conduit that fed the moat
from the Huay Kaeo stream off Doi Suthep — documented water engineering, not
just a name (Fine Arts Department, Office 7). The northwest is the high
corner; the whole square drinks from here. [Wat Lok Molee](/place/wat-lok-molee/)
stands on the outer bank a short walk east along the north wall.

## The wheel behind the corners

*Tradition, documented —* the ทักษาเมือง (*taksa mueang*) scheme reads the
city as born on a Thursday and lays the eight taksa qualities clockwise from
west: บริวาร retinue (W) → อายุ longevity (NW) → เดช power (N) → ศรี glory
(NE) → มูละ wealth (E) → อุตสาหะ industry (SE) → มนตรี counsel (S) →
กาลกิณี misfortune (SW), with เกตุ at the navel. The scheme is solidly
documented **as tradition** — and the scholarship holds it was formalized
*after* the founding era (Tilokarat and later), so it explains how the city
has been read for centuries, not necessarily what its founder intended in
1296. The corners inherit their characters from this wheel: ศรีภูมิ the
glory corner, กู่เฮือง the kalakini one — which is also why the customary
lap of the moat keeps the southwest quarter for transit rather than
destination.

## What the shelf says

Checked against this catalogue on 2026-08-27: **no manuscript title in the
collection names a corner** — not แจ่ง, not any of the four names, in any
script or romanisation the titles use. Zero is the finding, and it says
something true: the แจ่ง tradition is kept in brick, in rite, and in speech
— an architecture-and-observance tradition rather than a palm-leaf one. The
places where the corners should surface in writing are the city chronicles
([tamnan](/a?s=genre:tamnan_chronicle)) and the founding narratives — see
the open questions. The entity's aliases stand ready; the day a crawl
brings in a tamnan witness that names a แจ่ง, the counter above this
article will say so.

## Still alive

- The **June city-blessing at ten points** — five gates, four แจ่ง, the
  navel, fireworks-coordinated — runs annually under the municipality
  (documented in official announcements, 2025 and 2026).
- The **corpse-gate custom** persists: the dead do not leave by Chiang Mai
  Gate but by แสนปุง in the southwest, and residents observe it.
- Municipal **lamp-offerings at the four แจ่ง during Yi Peng** are reported
  in official material — a photograph with a date would firm this to solid.
- Street directions in the city still say แจ่ง unselfconsciously — hearing
  the word means the old city is near. The street-level companion to this
  article, with nine ways to tell inside from outside, lives at
  [motdang.net/moat.html](https://motdang.net/moat.html).
