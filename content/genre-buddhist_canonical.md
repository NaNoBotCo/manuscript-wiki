## What this is

This is the **Tipiṭaka** and its scholastic apparatus: the three baskets of the Pali
canon and the commentarial machinery a monastery needs to teach them. The catalogue's
raw labels lay the shape out plainly — **Sutta** (973 witnesses, the discourses),
**Vinaya** (494, monastic discipline), **Abhidhamma** (273, the systematic philosophy),
alongside a broad *General Buddhism* bucket (363) and **Anisong** (234), the
*ānisaṃsa* sermons on the merit that accrues from specific pious acts. The titles are
the backbone of Theravāda scholarship: *Samantapāsādika* (the great Vinaya commentary),
*mūlapaṇṇāsa majjhimanikāya*, *parivāra*, *pācittiya*, *Cakkavāḷadīpanī*.

At 2,350 witnesses this is the **largest single genre** in the collection — the devotional
and scholastic substrate on which everything else, including the *wichaa* material, was
copied. It is not itself research-priority, but it is the sea the priority genres swim in.

## A note on nissaya — why "canonical" understates it

Many of these witnesses are not bare Pali scripture. Titles beginning *Nisai / Nissaya*
(*Nisai athasalini*, *Nitsai katha thammapata*) are **nissaya** manuscripts: the Pali
text carried line-by-line with an interlinear gloss in Lan Na vernacular. The nissaya is
the characteristic form of Lanna Buddhist learning — it is where the intellectual labour
of the northern monasteries actually shows, the canon rendered *and construed* for
teaching. To file these simply as "canonical" undercounts how much of this genre is
local scholarship rather than transmitted scripture.

## The shape of the collection

Overwhelmingly **Tham Lanna** (85%) on **palm-leaf** (93%) — the canonical support in
its purest form, a bound stack copied for permanence rather than daily reference. The
dated witnesses run from the late 15th to the late 20th century (1495–1985), peaking hard
in the **19th century**, the great age of Lanna monastic copying.

The provincial picture reads as Phrae-dominant (45%), but that number belongs to one
library, not one region — see the note below.

## Notes

- **One collection drives the whole distribution.** **Wat Sung Men** in Phrae accounts
  for 1,014 of these 2,350 witnesses — **43% of the entire genre from a single temple**.
  Phrae's apparent dominance is really the survival and cataloguing of one extraordinary
  repository. Treat the province and temple counts here as a map of *where a great library
  was preserved*, not where canon was produced or used.
- **"Canonical" is a coarse bucket.** The three piṭaka, the commentaries, the *ānisaṃsa*
  merit-sermons and a large *General Buddhism* remainder all sit together. The most useful
  split would separate transmitted canon from **nissaya** (bilingual teaching copies) and
  from the para-canonical *anisong* — three quite different kinds of object under one heading.
- **Read alongside the birth-stories.** The *Khuddaka-nikāya* jātaka were pulled out into
  their own [Jātaka](/a?s=genre:jataka) genre, so this heading is the canon *minus* its
  most-copied narrative layer; the two are one corpus split by the taxonomy.
