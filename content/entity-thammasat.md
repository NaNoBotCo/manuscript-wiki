## What this is

The **thammasat** (ธรรมศาสตร์; Pali *dhammasattha*) is the **cosmic
law-treatise** — the text that tells a Southeast Asian legal world *where law
comes from and why it binds*. Its defining claim is startling if you take it
literally, and the tradition means it literally: law is **not made**. It is
older than kings, older than the world's present inhabitants, and it was
**found** — read off the boundary-wall at the edge of the universe by a sage
who went and looked.

It is the ***kadi tham*** pole of law — the dhamma-law — standing above and
legitimating the king's enforceable code, the
[Mangraisat](/a?s=entity:mangraisat). A Lanna judge worked between the two.

## The story, first — law read off the wall of the world

*Tradition holds —* the following is the narrative frame of the wider
Mon–Burmese–Tai dhammasattha literature, **not** established from the twenty
witnesses catalogued below. Treat it as orienting.

The origin story starts before there is anything to have law about. In the
Buddhist cosmology the treatises assume, the world dissolves and reforms in
enormous cycles, and the beings who repopulate it after each dissolution begin
as luminous, self-illuminating, weightless, without sex, hunger, or property.
Then one of them tastes the sweet earth-crust that has formed on the water.
The taste is the beginning of everything that follows: eating makes bodies
coarse, coarse bodies cast shadows and need shelter, difference appears,
appetite appears, and then — the moment the treatises are really about —
**someone takes rice from a plot that another had marked as theirs.** The
first theft in the history of the world. And because there is no procedure,
the injured party can do nothing but strike back.

So the beings assemble and elect the first king, the **Mahāsammata**, "the
Great Elect" — chosen by agreement to judge between them, paid a share of the
rice for doing it. *Tradition holds* the office begins as a **contract**, not
a conquest: the first monarch is a hired arbitrator.

But the king still has the problem of *by what standard*. And here the sage
enters. In the tellings, a seer of enormous attainment — named **Manu** in
most recensions, a minister or counsellor to the first king — travels to the
edge of the cosmos, to the great **cakkavāḷa** wall that encircles the
world-system, and finds the law **already written there in letters the size of
full-grown cattle**. He does not compose it. He copies it down, or memorises
it, and carries it back for humankind.

*Inference —* every consequence that matters follows from that single image. If
the law is inscribed on the wall of the universe, then a king who rules
against it is not making bad law — he is **misreading**, which is a different
and more dangerous kind of failure. The treatise arms the judge with a
standard his own ruler cannot rewrite. That is not a decorative myth; it is
the constitutional move of the whole tradition, and it is why the
[law shelf](/a?s=genre:law_customary) in this collection keeps the
cosmological book right next to the enforceable one.

*Tradition holds* the recensions differ on the sage's fate, and one strand is
worth recording for its honesty: in some tellings Manu, having delivered the
law, judges a case wrongly himself — and the error is visible, in some
versions to the point that he resigns or is unmasked. A legal literature that
builds a fallible transmitter into its own founding story is telling its users
something about how far to trust any single judgment.

## What the catalogue holds

**Twenty witnesses** carry a thammasat / dhammasat name, nearly all classed as
[customary law](/a?s=genre:law_customary). Set beside the **twenty-four**
[Mangraisat](/a?s=entity:mangraisat) witnesses, the catalogue holds **both
poles of the Thai legal cosmos in near-equal number** — a genuinely valuable
pairing.

The cluster sorts into a few named recensions, and the names are informative:

- **ธัมมสาดหลวง** *Thammasat luang*, "the great thammasat" — the full
  recension, at [Wat Chiang Man](/place/wat-chiang-man/) in Chiang Mai
  ([ms 803](/m/803/)) and twice at Wat Lom Raed in Lampang
  ([ms 4005](/m/4005/), dated 1817; [ms 4015](/m/4015/), dated 1895) — the
  same house copying it afresh nearly eighty years apart.
- **ธัมมสาตรอม** *Thammasat hom* — *hom* meaning gathered or compiled: an
  abridgement or digest ([ms 4006](/m/4006/) at Wat San Rim Ping, Lamphun,
  dated 1855; [ms 4287](/m/4287/)). Alongside it
  [ms 4475](/m/4475/) at Wat Nantharam pairs *วิไนรอม ธัมมสาด* — the
  **Vinaya** digest and the thammasat in one title.
- **ธัมมสาดคดีโลกคดีธัมม** *Thammasat kadi lok kadi tham* — the treatise
  presented explicitly as covering both poles at once ([ms 4012](/m/4012/) at
  Wat Ban Ueam, Lampang, dated 1841; [ms 5033](/m/5033/) from the same temple,
  dated 1947, a century later and classed
  [canonical](/a?s=genre:buddhist_canonical) rather than law).
- **ธัมมสาสตร์ราชสาสตร์** *Thammasat rachasat* ([ms 2031](/m/2031/) at
  [Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/)) — the **dhamma**-treatise and the
  **king's** treatise named as a matched pair, which is the whole architecture
  compressed into one title.

### The hinge title

One witness holds the entire structure in a single line.
[**ms 232**](/m/232/), from
[Wat Phra That Chang Kham](/place/wat-chang-kham-356063726/) in Nan and dated
1852, is catalogued as **คดีโลกคดีธัมม์ มังรายธัมมสาษ** — *Kadi lok kadi tham
manglai thammasat*: worldly cases, dhamma cases, **Mangrai**, **thammasat**.
The two poles and the founder-king's name, bound together on one bundle of
palm-leaf. And its cataloguers classed it not as law at all but as
[Buddhist canonical](/a?s=genre:buddhist_canonical).

*Inference —* that classification is not an error to correct; it is evidence.
A scribe in Nan in 1852 did not experience the cosmic frame and the king's
code as two genres that happened to be adjacent. He experienced them as one
subject, close enough to scripture that a cataloguer reading the bundle
reached for the canonical shelf. This single shelf-mark does more to
demonstrate the *kadi lok / kadi tham* architecture than any amount of
description, because nobody constructed it to make the point.

### Where it sits

The twenty witnesses spread **Lampang 5, Chiang Mai 5, Phrae 3, Phayao 3,
Lamphun 2** — with dated copies running **1817 to 1947**. *Inference —* two
things follow. First, the thammasat is a **lowland-valley text**: it clusters
in the old *müang* capitals with established monastic schools, and it is
thinner in Nan than the Mangrai law is, where the pattern reverses. The frame
stayed near the centres of learning while the enforceable code travelled to
the peripheries that needed it. Second, the 1947 copy is worth pausing on —
a treatise grounding law in a sage's reading of the cosmic wall was still
being hand-copied onto palm-leaf in a Lampang monastery two years after the
Second World War ended.

*Inference —* that the two clusters are of comparable size is itself a
finding: this collection preserves the *whole legal architecture*, not just
its enforceable half. The thammasat supplies the theory, the Mangraisat the
practice. Having both is what lets the archive show law as the tradition
itself understood it — a single structure with a heavenly frame and an
earthly body.

## The regional inheritance

*Tradition holds —* the dhammasattha is not a Thai invention. The literature
runs across the **Mon, Burmese and Tai** worlds, and the older strata are
Mon-Burmese; the Lanna thammasat is the northern Thai branch of a
**trans-regional** legal literature, which is part of why it recurs in so many
copies and why its recensions vary. The name and the wall-of-the-world image
are ultimately borrowed from the Sanskritic *dharmaśāstra* tradition — but the
borrowing is thorough rather than faithful. *Tradition holds* the Buddhist
recensions strip out the brahmanical caste ordering that organises the Indian
originals and re-found the whole treatise on the Mahāsammata contract instead:
law legitimated by an assembly's agreement rather than by birth. A borrowed
frame, rebuilt around a different theory of where authority begins.

## Notes

- **A well-attested anchor, best read as a pair.** The hard facts are *n = 20*
  and its near-parity with the Mangraisat's *n = 24*. The cosmological
  narrative above is marked tradition throughout; the cluster, the recension
  names, the provincial spread and [ms 232](/m/232/) are catalogue data.
- **No page-level text yet.** As with the whole
  [law genre](/a?s=genre:law_customary), none of the twenty has a transcribed
  page here — the Manu narrative is told from the wider tradition, not from
  these leaves. Transcribing one *Thammasat luang* would let this page quote
  its own witnesses instead.
- **Next move — the two-pole comparison.** *Inference —* the highest-value
  pass is reading the thammasat and [Mangraisat](/a?s=entity:mangraisat)
  witnesses **against each other**, surfacing how the discovered-law frame and
  the king's-law code cite and depend on one another inside this one
  collection. [ms 232](/m/232/), [ms 2031](/m/2031/) and
  [ms 2032](/m/2032/) — where both names share a title — are the natural
  starting points.
- **Read alongside.** The king whose code this frame legitimates is
  [Mangrai](/a?s=entity:mangrai); the maxims a ruler was advised by, rather
  than bound by, are the [Lokanīti](/a?s=entity:lokaniti). Browse the shelf at
  [Customary Law](/browse?genre=law_customary).
