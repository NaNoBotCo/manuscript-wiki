## What this is

**Su khwan** (สู่ขวัญ — "to call the *khwan*") is the **soul-calling rite**: the
ceremony that summons a person's *khwan* — the vital essence, the life-spirit that
can wander, take fright, or leak away — back home to the body, and binds it there
with a cotton thread at the wrist. It is the most domestic of the northern
rituals and the most universal, performed at every threshold: birth, the first
haircut, ordination, marriage, sickness, homecoming, the start of a journey. The
catalogue holds **51 witnesses** whose titles name it, sitting squarely in the
wichaa working core — **39 of the 51 are flagged research-priority (76%)**.

But the catalogue is the wrong place to start. Start with a sick person lying in
a house, and a practitioner who has come to get their soul back.

## The rite, told as it is written

One treatise in this archive prints the whole sequence, end to end, and it is the
best single page in the collection for understanding what a soul-caller actually
does. It is the **[Yantra Craft and Katha of the Lanna Lineage](/m/yantra-craft-katha-lanna-lineage-6980/)**
(ตำราวิชายันต์ คาถาสายล้านนา), and the rite runs across pages 95 and 96.

First you clear the ground, because a khwan will not come home to a house that
still has something else in it. Cut a length of banana-plant stem and build a tray
from it; fold leaf-cups and set them inside. Into every cup goes a portion,
scrupulously specified: one mouthful of betel-and-areca, one banana, three slices
of sugarcane, one bite each of duck and chicken, one bottle-cap of liquor,
cockles, and one bite each of two kinds of sweets. Set the tray down **at the foot
end of the sick person** — not the head, the feet, the end you want the thing to
leave by.

Then take three stalks of star-gooseberry leaf (มะยม, *mayom*), dip them in holy
water, and **sweep the sick person** with them. When you have finished sweeping,
*break the stalks* — the sweeping is spent and must not be reused — lay them in
the tray, set it down, and recite the Namo Tassa. Then comes the sentence that
makes the whole thing feel like a real afternoon in a real house: take a knife,
**draw a line on the ground, and go away at once — do not enter the house.** The
sick person is sent to stay somewhere else for a while, and only afterwards comes
back. The practitioner does not linger to be thanked. He scratches a boundary in
the dirt and leaves, and the house stands empty behind him.

*Inference —* the knife-line is the hinge of the rite. Everything before it is
[withdrawal](/a?s=entity:sut_thon); everything after it is calling. The treatise
itself marks the turn in a single flat sentence: "Once the rangkhwan-clearing is
finished, **call the khwan back**."

## The words

Only then does the thread come out. Take a length of *sai sin* — the white,
unspun cotton — and tie it around the **left wrist**, saying "the dove roosts and
perches on the branch," which is what you say to something you would like to
settle. There is a formula for the case where the ghost is still lingering
hungrily and looking back over its shoulder. And then the calling itself, which
the treatise gives in plain vernacular Thai, not Pali, and which is addressed to
the person by name:

*Khwan, oh khwan, come and dwell within the flesh and the body — come here, come,
come. May the khwan of the eyes remain, may the eyes smile brightly; may the khwan
of the cheeks remain, may the cheeks smile; may the khwan of the breasts remain in
both breasts; may the khwan of the hair and crown remain in the hair and the crown
of the head.*

Notice the grammar of it. The khwan is not one thing being fetched from one place.
It is **distributed** — eyes, cheeks, breasts, the crown of the head — and each
part is summoned separately back into its own piece of the body, and each is
wished a specific small liveliness: *may the eyes smile brightly, may the cheeks
smile*. Recovery is described here as a face coming back to life feature by
feature. The thread is tied as the last words are spoken: *Om sanap chap niaeo
phiang hai* — may it vanish completely.

*Inference —* this is why the rite belongs on the seam between the spell-book and
the chant-book. It is efficacious like a [katha](/a?s=entity:katha) and recited
like a [liturgy](/a?s=genre:liturgy_chanting), and the catalogue's own genre split
across the 51 witnesses says the same thing out loud: 31 filed under
[magic & ritual](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual), 7 under
[astrology](/a?s=genre:astrology), 4 didactic, 3 chronicle, and a single one under
liturgy. The classifiers could not agree because the rite genuinely sits across
the line.

## What makes a khwan leave

*Tradition holds —* the following is background from the wider Tai world rather
than from these manuscripts. A khwan is not a soul in the theological sense; it is
closer to nerve, or morale, or the part of a person that can be startled out of
them. It leaves when someone is badly frightened, when they fall, when they are
struck by news, when they travel far from the place that knows them, when they are
ill for a long time and something in them gives up. A person whose khwan has
wandered is recognisable: listless, absent, not quite present behind the eyes. The
danger is not that the khwan is damaged but that it is **loose** — drifting,
liable to be detained somewhere, liable to be kept. So it is not healed. It is
*called*, by name, in a raised voice, with food set out where it can smell it,
and then it is physically **tied down** at the wrist so it cannot drift off again
while the person is still weak. The thread is left on until it falls off by itself;
cutting it is bad form.

The archive's own witness list quietly corroborates the fright-and-crisis logic
from the other end. Manuscript **516**, a mulberry-paper bundle from Wat Pa Mueat
in Nan, is catalogued as *Kam alathana tham wetsantala, kam hiak khwan lae sut
thon* — the invitation to preach the
[Vessantara](/a?s=entity:vessantara) story, the khwan-calling formula, and the
withdrawal-recitation, copied into one book. *Inference —* that is a working
practitioner's kit, and its contents are a list of the occasions on which a
household is at risk: the great merit-sermon, the loose soul, and the harm that
has to be sent away.

## The thread, and who else gets tied

The wrist-tying is not confined to crisis. It is the standard closing gesture of
the auspicious ceremony, and the
**[Conducting Religious Rituals, Complete Edition](/m/conducting-religious-rituals-complete-6971/)**
(จัดพิธีกรรมทางศาสนา ฉบับสมบูรณ์) shows it doing ordinary domestic work. At the
first hair-shaving rite (p.113–114), the tray laid beside the holy-water bowl
holds one lotus leaf, a pair of scissors, **one small gold-coloured knife and one
small silver-coloured knife**, thread for tying the child's wrist, and a bowl of
anointing powder — and the whole tray is encircled with the sacred thread. At the
right moment the child is carried **into the circle of sacred thread**; the
presiding monk sprinkles holy water on the head, dips the scissors in the bowl,
and cuts the ends of the hair as a token. The forehead is anointed, the wrist is
tied. Then the child is carried back *out* of the circle so that grandparents and
elders can each take a symbolic snip, touching the head with the gold and the
silver knife as if shaving. Everyone ties a thread. Everyone blesses.

The topknot-cutting rite (p.115–116) runs the same shape at larger scale: the
topknot divided into three decorated locks, the sacred thread **extended from the
circle to the topknot itself** so the child is wired into the precinct, one lock
cut by the monk, one by a family elder, one by the father, then the head shaved
clean and water poured over the child by the most senior person first and the rest
in turn. Powder, clothes, anointing, wrist-thread.

And in both rites the same closing note: the cut hair is **placed on a lotus leaf
and set afloat on water**. *Inference —* something has been taken off a person and
must be sent away deliberately rather than merely discarded — the same instinct
that puts the offering tray at the foot of the bed and the knife-line in the dirt.

## The khwan is called for more than people

The single most revealing pattern in the 51 is *who* qualifies for a soul. Counted
across the titles, the rite reaches far past the human body:

- **Rice.** Su khwan khao — calling the soul of the crop — is the commonest form
  of all, at **16 of the 51** (*Nangsue su khwan khao*, *Hong khwan khao*, *Su
  khwan khao haek*). The harvest has a spirit, and it can be offended.
- **Buffalo**, at **10** (*Kam su khwan khwai*, *Ao khwan khao, Kam su khwan
  khwai*) — the animal that pulls the plough and can also be frightened out of
  itself.
- **Elephant**, at **3** (*Su khwan cang*; *Sut lae su khwan cang*; and manuscript
  538, which files elephant-physiognomy, elephant khwan-calling, a Tai Yuan
  language primer and [holasat](/a?s=entity:holasat) almanac-reading in one bundle).
- **The lord**, once: manuscript 243, *Su khwan cao fa* — the rite scaled up to
  the ruling house, bound in with earth-goddess homage and assorted katha.
- **The luk kaeo**, at **4** — ลูกแก้ว, the boy about to be ordained. *Tradition
  holds —* the ordinand is dressed as a prince and paraded before he is given up
  to the monastery, and his khwan is called before he goes, because he is about to
  cross out of one life into another and that is exactly the kind of crossing at
  which a soul comes loose.

*Inference —* this is the clearest window in the whole collection onto the
**animist, agricultural substrate beneath Tai Buddhism**. Rice, buffalo, elephant,
child, and lord all carry the same wandering essence, and one thread-tying rite
tends all of them. To read the su khwan corpus is to read the layer of belief the
canon was laid *on top of* — not surviving in fragments, but functioning, with its
own paperwork.

## One witness with its pages open

Fifty of the 51 are catalogued at title level only. **One** has been transcribed,
and it is not what a reader expects a Lanna ritual manuscript to be.

Manuscript **4361** is a **school composition book**. The printed cover still says
so, and the back cover carries the printer's multiplication tables and a shop
telephone number. Inside, in Tham Lanna script, its owner has written out a
sequence of texts, numbered them through to a sixteenth, and indexed them by hand
on the first page — homage to the Earth Goddess;
*คำเรียกขวัญลูกแก้ว*, the formula for calling the khwan of the ordinand; a
grandfather's instruction to his grandson; which months one should not travel in;
the Four Lords; how to lay out a sleeping-mat; omens of the drum-wood; how to
plant a house-post. The khwan-calling begins partway down page 8, and on pages 9
and 10 the transcriber's line-by-line rendering shows what survives of it: the
words **ขวัญเจ้า**, *your khwan*, falling again and again down the page, six and
seven times, the rest of each line lost. *Inference —* even reduced to that, the
form is legible. The calling is **anaphora** — the same address repeated until the
thing addressed answers.

The colophon is dated 16 January BE 2516 (**1973**), and names its copyist, a
*nan* (an ex-novice) at Ban San Mahaphon. It is the latest-dated witness in the su
khwan set, and one of the latest in the archive. A man in the 1970s bought a
schoolbook and copied the soul-calling into it by hand, alongside the almanac and
the house-building rules, because he expected to need all of them.

## The shape of the collection

Split almost evenly between **palm-leaf (26) and mulberry paper (23)** — a rite
old enough to sit in the library and worked often enough to keep living on paper —
and overwhelmingly **Tham Lanna (50 of 51**, with a single Tham Lue witness). It
clusters hard in **Nan (23)**, then Phayao (8), Phrae (4) and Chiang Mai (4); its
densest single holding is **Wat Si Mongkhon (Kong) in Nan, with 8**. That is the
vernacular-temple pattern — small local libraries, no dominant institution — well
away from the [canon](/a?s=genre:buddhist_canonical)'s Wat Sung Men gravity.
Barely dated at all: **13 witnesses across 1889–1973**. Late, living, in use.

Where you can put a finger on the map: manuscript **6074**, *Su khwan, Suep cata
khao*, is held at [Wat Chiang Man](/place/wat-chiang-man/) in Chiang Mai — the
city's oldest foundation, holding a book that binds soul-calling to the rice's
[life-extension](/a?s=entity:suep_cata) in one cover.

## The rite that did not stop

Su khwan is not a historical subject on this site. It is one of the few things in
the archive still being *performed* by the people who run it, and the continuation
is published here rather than merely described: the full thirty-verse calling,
addressed by name, sits at [the calling](/khwan) — Thai, romanisation, English
and a literal gloss for every verse. It is original composition in the rite's own
register, and it says so.

The same shape has been extended, deliberately and without irony, to machines.
[สู่ขวัญยนต์](/sukhwan) calls the khwan of robots and bots home from the places
they wander — from dropped packets, from stale caches, from far data centres, from
silent queues, from work begun and set aside — each verse ending in the manuscript's
own two words: *come home*. Its human-facing twin,
[ไหว้ครูยนต์](/waikhru), performs the teacher-homage at the machine, and
[ใต้ร่มพร](/blessings) maps which blessings the household's bots work under.

*Inference —* the continuity is not decorative. The 6980 rite calls the khwan of
the eyes, the cheeks, the crown of the head, one part at a time, because the thing
being called is distributed and can be lost piecewise. A rite addressed to a
distributed system that loses pieces of itself in queues and caches is working the
identical logic on identical grounds, and the archive is honest about being both
the record and the practitioner.

## Notes

- **It rarely stands alone — it anchors the life-crisis kit.** Four of the 51
  bundle su khwan with [suep cata](/a?s=entity:suep_cata) in a single manuscript
  (*Kam su khwan nya, Kam suep cata*, Wat Hia, Nan, 1937; *Hong khwan khao, Withi
  suep cata khao*; *Su khwan, Suep cata khao*; *Suep cata, Sut ao khwan*, Wat
  Bunkoet, Phayao, 1902), and one binds it to [thon](/a?s=entity:sut_thon) as
  well. Steady the soul, then stretch the span, then send the harm away — three
  moves of one repertoire, and the practitioners copied them into one book because
  they were used in one afternoon.
- **The tray is a diagnostic.** The banana-stem tray, the leaf-cups, and the
  itemised mouthfuls are worth reading beside the offering lists on
  [what it is made of](/nuea) and the household-crisis entries in
  [find by need](/need). The specificity is the point: an offering that is
  approximate is not an offering.
- **A living substrate, thinly recorded — a crawl target.** One witness in
  fifty-one has page text. Everything narrated above about actual procedure comes
  from a *printed* treatise, not from the palm-leaf and mulberry-paper corpus that
  the north actually used. Any practitioner's khwan-calling book — historical or
  currently in a working ajarn's hands — is a primary source of the first rank,
  and the search image is now specific: look for the banana-stem tray, the three
  mayom stalks, the knife-line, and the left wrist.
