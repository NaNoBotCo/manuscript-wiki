## What this is

**Tamnan** is the northern Thai chronicle: history told as sacred etiology. A tamnan
explains how something holy came to be *here* — how a relic reached a hill, how an
image was cast, how the Buddha's teaching arrived in a particular muang, how a lineage
or a polity began. It is the *vaṃsa* literature of Lanna, cousin to the Sinhalese
chronicle tradition, and its titles in this collection are exactly that: *Samaeng that
to Suthep* (the chronicle of the relic of Doi Suthep), the *Anāgatavaṃsa* (the chronicle
of the future Buddha, Metteyya), a run of *…watthu* origin-legends, and the "sixteen
muang" (*sip hok moeng*) polity-chronicles.

Read the raw labels before assuming "chronicle" means dynastic annals. The catalogue
splits this genre as **Buddhist Chronicle (54%)** and **Tamnan (31%)** against only
**15% "Secular History."** This is overwhelmingly the *religious* chronicle — history
in the service of a relic, an image or the *sāsana* — rather than the courtly annal
(*phongsāwadān*) the word "chronicle" might suggest. That religious weight shows in the
language too: the Pali content runs heavy here, because a *vaṃsa* is written in, or
construed against, the canonical tongue.

For this archive this genre is the **connective tissue**. Tamnan are not *wichaa*, but
they are where the sacred landscape is documented and legitimised — the relics, images,
lineages and places that the living amulet-and-relic culture still draws its authority
from. A chronicle is often the earliest paperwork a holy object has.

## The shape of the collection

At 1,060 witnesses this is a large genre, and — unusually — a **genuinely pan-Lanna**
one. Where the canonical corpus is really one Phrae library, the chronicle spreads
evenly: **Phrae (24%) and Chiang Mai (23%) stand almost level**, with Phayao and Lampang
close behind. That Chiang Mai — the old capital — figures so strongly is telling; so is
the appearance of **Wat Chiang Man** (the first royal temple of the city) and **Wat Si
Khom Kham** (Phayao) among the top holdings alongside the ever-present Wat Sung Men. The
place-counts here carry real regional signal, not just the shadow of a single collection.

Its physical support is canonical — **palm-leaf (93%)**, **Tham Lanna (94%)** — the
chronicle copied for permanence like the scripture it accompanies, not as a working paper.

## Notes

- **Religious chronicle, not secular annal.** The genre is dominated by *vaṃsa* — relic
  histories, image histories, *sāsana* histories — with dynastic and muang history a
  minority. Separating the Buddhist chronicle from the secular *phongsāwadān* is the
  obvious next sub-taxonomy, and the one that would matter most to a historian.
- **The most even geography of the large genres.** Unlike the canonical bulk, no single
  temple dominates: Wat Sung Men still leads (145) but holds only ~14% here, and Chiang
  Mai's royal-temple material gives this genre a breadth the others lack. Provenance
  counts in this genre are worth trusting as a regional picture.
- **Cross-border witnesses.** A small but real **Shan** (32) and **Tham Lao** (28) presence,
  with *Pali-and-Shan* and *Pali-and-Lao* language pairs, marks where the chronicle
  tradition travelled across the wider Tai Buddhist world. Worth flagging for anyone
  reconstructing the borderland transmission.
- **Copying range.** The dated witnesses run from the 16th to the 21st century, weighted
  hard toward the 19th and 20th — a chronicle was recopied as long as its relic or muang
  still mattered, so the genre stays alive later than the strictly canonical corpora.
