## What this is

**Tamnan** (ตำนาน) is the northern chronicle, and at **1,060 witnesses** it is the
third-largest genre in this archive — behind only the
[canon](/a?s=genre:buddhist_canonical) and the [jātaka](/a?s=genre:jataka). It is
also the most *narrative* body of writing the north produced about itself. These
are the stories the muang told to explain why the ground under them was holy: how
a relic reached a particular hill, why a queen came up a particular river, which
king the Buddha personally prophesied, and what happened to the city that ate the
wrong fish.

Six hundred and seventeen of the 1,060 titles literally begin ตำนาน or ทำนาน —
"the tamnan of." A hundred and seventy-six name a **ธาตุ** (*that*, a relic or the
stupa holding it). A hundred and twenty-four name a **เมือง** (*muang*, a
polity). Forty-seven carry **วงส์** (*vaṃsa*, lineage). That distribution is the
genre's whole argument in miniature: relic, place, descent. Everything else is
commentary.

## The form: history with the sacred as its engine

A tamnan is not a chronicle that happens to mention miracles. It is a chronicle in
which the miracle *is the causal mechanism*. Ask a tamnan why a dynasty rules from
a particular hill and it will not answer with a battle or a marriage. It will
answer: because a relic came to rest there, and the relic chose.

This is worth stating plainly, because it is what makes the genre legible. In a
tamnan the ordinary machinery of history — armies, succession, taxation — is
present but demoted. It is the *effect* of the sacred fact, not the cause of it.
The Buddha walks a valley and prophesies; centuries later a king who obeys the
prophecy prospers and a king who ignores it does not. A relic is enshrined; the
polity that houses it becomes legitimate, and the neighbouring polity that wants
legitimacy has to acquire a relic of its own. Whole wars in these texts are, at
bottom, arguments about custody of bone.

*Inference —* this is why the catalogue's own three-way split of the genre keeps
leaking. The raw labels are [Buddhist Chronicle](/a?s=subgenre:tamnan_chronicle:Buddhist Chronicle) (569),
plain [Tamnan](/a?s=subgenre:tamnan_chronicle:Tamnan) (328), and
[Secular History](/a?s=subgenre:tamnan_chronicle:Secular History) (159) — but a
genre built on sacred causation cannot cleanly yield a secular half. The
"secular" shelf here holds the *Pali Cāmadevīvaṃsa* (ms 1350, Wat Sung Men, 1833),
whose heroine is invited to found a city by a hermit; it holds eight copies of
**ปฐมมูลมูลี**, a cosmogony; it holds seven of **ปฐมกัป**, the first aeon. Those
are not secular documents. They are documents the cataloguer had nowhere else to
put. The split is real as a *labelling* fact and soft as a content fact, and the
three sub-pages take that up in detail.

## The Buddha walked here

The master narrative of the Lanna tamnan is **พระเจ้าเลียบโลก** — *Phra Chao Liap
Lok*, "the Buddha circuits the world." The collection holds **16 witnesses** of it,
copied at Wat Chiang Man in Chiang Mai (ms 776, 1834; ms 788; ms 792), at Wat Sung
Men in Phrae (ms 2010, 1875), at Wat Na Pang in Nan (ms 638, 1815), at Wat Si Khom
Kham in Phayao (ms 3511, 1933), at Wat Ban Laeo in Chiang Rai (ms 3510, 1919) — the
same text recopied in seven provinces across a century and a half, which is what a
foundational narrative looks like in a manuscript catalogue.

*Tradition holds —* the story is a walking tour. The Buddha, with Ānanda beside
him, travels north out of the Ganges plain and up the valleys of what will become
Lanna, and at each stop a local — a Lua hillman, a hunter, a farmer, in some
tellings animals, a crow, a pair of mice — offers him something small and poor.
He accepts it, smiles, and gives back a hair from his head, or presses a footprint
into rock, or simply says: after my parinibbāna, a relic of mine will come to rest
in this place, and there will be a great city here. The genre's entire premise is
loaded into that sentence. Every relic-stupa in the north can then be read as the
prophecy coming true on schedule, and every tamnan of a *that* is a receipt.

The corpus keeps the companion pieces too: **19** chronicle titles name a
**พระบาท** (*phra bat*, the footprint), **12** a **เกสาธาตุ** (*kesa dhātu*, the
hair-relic). Ms 780 at [Wat Chiang Man](/place/wat-chiang-man/) (1812) is titled
*Tamnan pha cao wai pha bat pha that* — the chronicle of the Buddha paying homage
to footprint and relic — and ms 6794 at Wat Mae Wa Luang, Lampang, files
**ตำนานแม่ระมิงค์**, the chronicle of the Ping river, explicitly as *phuk* 9 of the
Liap Lok. The river gets its own fascicle of the Buddha's itinerary. That is how
completely this text organises northern geography.

## The relics that have chronicles

Because each *that* generated its own paperwork, the relic-tamnan double as a map.
The [atlas](/atlas) and the [wat gazetteer](/wats) can be read against this list:

- **พระธาตุดอยสุเทพ** — Doi Suthep, **10 witnesses**, and not one of them from
  Doi Suthep: ms 6046 was copied at
  [Wat Phra That Hariphunchai](/place/wat-phra-that-hariphunchai-woramahawihan/)
  in Lamphun in 1920, ms 3588 at Wat Mueang Mo in Phrae in 1884 (titled
  *Tamnan pha mahacinathat cao sutep*, the Victor's Relic on Suthep), ms 5985 at
  Wat Luang Ratchasanthan in Phayao in 1909. A famous relic's chronicle travels
  further than the relic does.
  *Tradition holds —* the famous version: a relic brought north by the monk Sumana
  splits in two at Wat Suan Dok; the second half is loaded onto a white elephant,
  which is turned loose, climbs the mountain, trumpets three times, circles three
  times, kneels, and dies on the spot — and there the stupa goes. Note who decides:
  not the king, the elephant. The mountain itself is named for a hermit, Wāsudeva,
  which is why the [lersi](/a?s=entity:lersi) are never far from a Lanna
  foundation story. See [Wat Phra That Doi Suthep](/place/wat-phra-that-doi-suthep/).
- **พระธาตุดอยตุง** — Doi Tung, **13 witnesses**, several tying it explicitly to
  the old capital: ms 2750 and ms 3586, both *Tamnan that doi tung mueang ciang
  saen*, copied at Wat Mueang Mo in 1884; ms 6849 at
  [Wat Si Khom Kham](/place/wat-si-khom-kham-phayao/) in 1960.
  *Tradition holds —* the name is the ตุง, the banner: a flag of enormous length
  was raised on the summit and every acre its shadow crossed was given to the
  shrine. Land tenure decided by a shadow. See
  [Wat Phra That Doi Tung](/place/wat-phra-that-doi-tung/).
- **พระธาตุหริภุญชัย** — Lamphun, **17 witnesses** under its several spellings
  (หริภุญ, ละพูร, ลพูน, รภุญ), including ms 3790 at Wat Duang Di, Chiang Mai
  (1861) and ms 6708 at [Wat Chiang Man](/place/wat-chiang-man/) (1833).
  *Tradition holds —* the relic lay buried under the royal latrine until a king
  who learned of it moved his palace rather than the bone. See
  [Wat Phra That Hariphunchai](/place/wat-phra-that-hariphunchai-woramahawihan/).
- **พระธาตุช่อแฮ** — Phrae's own, **7 witnesses**, all from Phrae temples,
  the earliest ms 6638 at Wat Phra Luang Sung Men dated **1780**. A muang keeps
  its own relic's paperwork at home. See
  [Wat Phra That Cho Hae](/place/wat-phra-that-cho-hae/).
- **พระธาตุแช่แห้ง** — Nan, **5 witnesses**, ms 782 copied at Wat Chiang Man in
  1815 and ms 6767 at Wat Nong Bua, Nan, in 1883, the latter pairing Chae Haeng
  with Phu Phiang, the hill it stands on. See
  [Wat Phra That Chae Haeng](/place/wat-phra-that-chae-haeng/).
- **พระธาตุลำปางหลวง** — and here the catalogue does something lovely. Ms 4912,
  held at [Wat Phra That Lampang Luang](/place/wat-phra-that-lampang-luang/)
  itself and dated **1713**, is titled **จารึกตำนานมหาธาตุ** — *the inscription of
  the chronicle of the great relic*: a palm-leaf copy of a stone inscription of a
  relic chronicle, kept at the relic. Three media deep, and it never left home.

Beyond bone, the genre chronicles **images**: 23 titles name the **พระแก้ว**, the
crystal or emerald Buddha, and the cluster around **วัดพระแก้วดอนเต้า** at Lampang
(ms 777, ms 2254, ms 3558, ms 3559, ms 6594, ms 6797 and more) keeps calling the
city by its old name **เมืองกุกุตะนคอร**, Kukkuṭanagara — the rooster city, which
is where the north's obsession with the [kai](/a?s=entity:kai) touches its
chronicles. See [Wat Phra Kaeo Don Tao](/place/wat-phra-kaeo-don-tao/).

## The queen who was sent for

The other great founding story in the collection has a woman at the centre of it.
**นางจามเทวี** — Camadevi of Haripuñjaya — carries **nine witnesses**, and they
scatter across all three sub-genre labels at once, which tells you how little the
labels mean here. The Pali *Cāmadevīvaṃsa* (ms 1350) and *Pali cammatewi*
(ms 1331) were both copied at [Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/), in 1833 and
1842, and both are filed *Secular History*; ms 201 at Wat Phra That Chang Kham,
Nan (1869) and ms 4167, *Tamnan mae cao mae cam tewi* (1964), are filed *Buddhist
Chronicle*; ms 6710 at [Wat Pratu Pa](/place/wat-pratu-pa/), Lamphun (1928) and
ms 6880 at Wat Mae Kham Nam Lat, Chiang Rai (1953) are plain *Tamnan*. One queen,
three shelves, a hundred and thirty years of continuous recopying — the latest of
them made when the Second World War was in living memory.

*Tradition holds —* a hermit builds a city in the Ping valley and finds he has no
one to rule it, so he sends downriver to Lavo (Lopburi) for a princess. Camadevi
comes north by boat, pregnant, at the head of a convoy carrying monks, scribes,
physicians, sculptors and metalworkers — the story is quite specific that what
founds a city is not an army but a staffed institution. She bears twin sons and
splits the north between them. The tamnan is calmly clear that the ruler of the
new polity is a woman, that she was recruited, and that her mandate came from an
ascetic rather than a battlefield.

The founding stories keep the same shape at city scale: **27** titles name Chiang
Mai, **33** name Chiang Saen. Ms 6593 is *tāṃnān nabapurī mīöṅ biṅ jīeṅ hmaï1* —
the tamnan of Nopburi, the muang of the Ping, Chiang Mai — the city under the
ceremonial name [Mangrai](/a?s=entity:mangrai) gave it. Where a tamnan narrates a
founder it stands one step from the charter that legitimises him, which is why the
[Mangrai code](/a?s=entity:mangraisat) and the
[customary law](/a?s=genre:law_customary) shelf are this genre's closest
neighbours — and why two manuscripts titled **ตำนานพญามังราย**, "the tamnan of
Phaya Mangrai" (ms 641, Wat Na Pang, Nan; ms 5574, Wat Si Khom Kham), sit in the
catalogue under *Law* rather than here.

## Where the tamnan becomes wichaa

The palm-leaf chronicles in this catalogue are title-level records — no
transcribed text (see Notes). But the contributed printed treatises *do* have full
text, and one of them shows exactly what the wichaa tradition does with a
chronicle. The [Supreme Maha Mantra Katha](/m/supreme-maha-mantra-katha-6968/)
runs a section headed "Great and Valiant Heroes Who Excelled Supremely in Mantras
and Incantations" (p.55), and it is pure tamnan, retold as a case record.

Its first hero is **พระยาเจือง**, Phaya Chueang — the same figure this catalogue
holds **10 chronicle witnesses** of. The treatise gives him a birth year, a
prophecy that he would be a cakravartin, three strategic marriages won by turning
up with an elephant-catching expedition, and then the line that matters to a katha
book: "supremely skilled in warfare and possessing powerful mantras and great
yantras for his protection, [he] could not be touched by any weapon on his body,
not even once, even in battle" (p.56). The chronicle is being read as proof that
[katha](/a?s=entity:katha) and [yant](/a?s=entity:yantra) work. The treatise even
notes the story's spread — "the legend of Thao Hung–Thao Chueang, an epic of the
Lao; and the legend of Chao Chueang Han of the Tai Lue of Sipsong Panna."

Its second hero gets four pages and the best scene in the corpus. **Nan Thip
Chang** (pp.57–60): Burmese forces hold Lamphun, Chiang Saen and Chiang Rai;
Lampang has no lord, only four officials too busy fighting each other to fight
anyone else. Three abbots disrobe to raise a rising and are shot down — the abbot
of Wat Na Yang taking a bullet between the eyebrows, his two lieutenants falling
as they run to catch him. The victorious Thao Mahayot quarters his army *inside*
[Wat Phra That Lampang Luang](/place/wat-phra-that-lampang-luang/) and taxes the
district at spearpoint. A senior monk at Wat Phra Kaeo Chomphu then does the most
tamnan thing imaginable: he consults the magical texts to find out who is supposed
to save the city, and the texts name a forest hunter, Nan Thip Chang. Thip Chang
agrees — but first extracts a public pledge that if he wins, the city is his.

He takes 300 men, arrives at the temple in the dead of night, and goes in alone
through the western drainage channel — "which still exists today" — disguised as a
courier carrying a letter from Thao Mahayot's own consort. Thao Mahayot is in the
Wihan Luang playing chess with his officer and his concubine. Thip Chang hands
over the letter, steps back a measured distance, and shoots him dead across the
chessboard; the ball passes through and strikes the iron railing around the
reliquary, "a mark of which still remains today" (p.60). He leaves by the drain he
came in by. The townspeople and the monk crown him with lustral water in 1732, and
he founds the dynasty this catalogue holds the chronicle of — **ตำนานเจ้าเจ็ดตน**,
the Seven Lords (ms 4039 and ms 5572 at Wat Si Khom Kham; ms 6599).

*Inference —* note the two "still remains today" clauses. That is the tamnan's
characteristic move: the narrative terminates in a physical mark a reader can go
and put a finger on. A drainage channel, a dent in an iron rail, a footprint in
rock, a hill where an elephant knelt. The story is not asking to be believed. It
is asking to be *visited* — which is why this genre and the
[atlas](/atlas) are the same project.

The same treatise section goes on to the love-charm worked by Pu Chao Sa-ming
Phrai in the *Lilit Phra Lo*, a flying betel-quid sent across two hostile muang
onto a prince's dais (pp.60–61), and then to the origin-legend behind the
**ม้าเสพนาง** yantra (pp.62–63) — proof that in a wichaa manual, chronicle,
[literature](/a?s=genre:poetry_literary) and
[ritual instruction](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual) are one continuous kind of evidence.
The [Akhom of Phra Narai](/m/akhom-magic-king-narai-era-6970/) does it more
briefly, teaching an invisibility mantra as "the mantra the novice monk used when
he went down to see the elder Upagutta, who keeps the rains-retreat in the
[nāga](/a?s=entity:naga) realm" (p.169) — a chronicle-figure cited as the warrant
for a working spell.

## The shape of the collection

At 1,060 witnesses across **114 named temples**, this is the most **genuinely
pan-Lanna** of the large genres. Where the canonical corpus is really one Phrae
library, the chronicle spreads: **Phrae 259** and **Chiang Mai 249** stand almost
level, with Phayao (144) and Lampang (118) close behind, then Lamphun (63), Nan
(57), Chiang Rai (54), Mae Hong Son (39). [Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/)
still leads at temple level with 145 — but that is only 14% of the genre, against
its crushing dominance elsewhere; [Wat Si Khom Kham](/place/wat-si-khom-kham-phayao/)
holds 88, Wat Mueang Mo 47, [Wat Pa Sak Noi](/place/wat-pa-sak-noi/) 41,
[Wat Chiang Man](/place/wat-chiang-man/) — Mangrai's first royal temple in Chiang
Mai — 39. Place-counts in this genre carry real regional signal.

Its physical support is canonical: **palm-leaf 986 of 1,060 (93%)**, **Tham Lanna
996 (94%)**, only 40 on mulberry paper. The chronicle was copied for permanence,
like the scripture it accompanies, not as a working paper. **684 witnesses carry a
date**, running from **1574** — ms 4421, a *Pathama sampothi* at Wat Lai Hin
Luang, Lampang — to **2024**, ms 6201 at Wat Li, Phayao, which is the most recent
witness in the entire archive. A chronicle got recopied for as long as its relic
or its muang still mattered, and evidently one still did in 2024.

Language marks the borderland too: alongside 653 *Pali and Lan Na* and 327
*Pali-Northern Thai*, there are **32 Shan** and **28 Tham Lao** witnesses, plus
*Pali and Shan* (20), *Pali and Lao* (16) and even *Pali, Shan and Burmese* (10) —
the chronicle travelling across the wider Tai Buddhist world. The Sinhalese
ancestry is in the shelf itself: **12 copies of the ถูปวงส์** (*Thūpavaṃsa*, the
chronicle of the stupa) and three of the **มหาวงส์** (*Mahāvaṃsa*) sit here, the
Ceylon model the Lanna *vaṃsa* was built on.

## Two catalogues wearing one genre

One structural fact governs every count above. This genre is fed by exactly two
crawled sources, and they do not overlap in labelling at all: **DLNTM** supplies
all 569 *Buddhist Chronicle*, all 159 *Secular History* and the four odd ones out;
**EFEO** supplies all 328 plain *Tamnan*, and nothing else. The three sub-genres
are, in part, two cataloguing houses talking past each other. They *do* overlap in
the field: 20 of EFEO's 44 temples are also DLNTM temples, and at Wat Chiang Man
the same bundles are demonstrably catalogued twice — ms 787 and ms 6708 are both
the Lamphun great-relic tamnan dated 1833; ms 782 and ms 6703 are both Chae Haeng
1815; ms 790 and ms 6705 are both Chom Yong 1877. Read the
[Tamnan sub-page](/a?s=subgenre:tamnan_chronicle:Tamnan) before adding any two
sub-genre figures together.

## Notes

- **Title-level only — this is the crawl priority.** Of the 1,060 chronicle
  witnesses, **two** have any page records at all: ms 3476 and ms 4361, 29 pages
  between them. The most narrative genre in the archive currently contains no
  narrative. Every story on this page is either a title, or comes from a printed
  treatise, or is marked *Tradition holds —*. Transcribing even one full
  *Phra Chao Liap Lok* would change what this archive can say more than any other
  single act of work.
- **And both exceptions prove the point.** A transcription pass over ms 3476,
  ตำนานท้าวทั้งสี่, found four DLNTM microfilm contact-sheet frames photographing
  several palm-leaf strips at once, with body text around 20–25 px of glyph height
  — below the threshold for reading. What *was* legible was the annotation layer:
  a title board reading "๕๖๖. ตำนานท้าวทั้ง ๔ (๑ ผูก) … อ. หางดง จ. เชียงใหม่ …
  13 หน้า", one fascicle, seven leaves. The same pass turned up a folder-integrity
  problem worth knowing about: two of the four frames are a *different*
  manuscript, ๓๙๓. ตำนานพระยาธัมมิกราช from Lamphun. Nothing of the storyline —
  no name or direction of the four lords — is recoverable at this resolution, and
  none should be cited. Higher-resolution images are the blocker.
  Ms 4361, meanwhile, is not a chronicle at all: a paper Composition Book school
  notebook in blue ballpoint, 45 pages, dated by its own colophon to 16–18 January
  1973, holding sixteen sections of house-building and life-cycle rite — ปูชาแม่ธรณี,
  เรียกขวัญ, ท้าวทัง ๔, จักฝังเสาเรือน. It sits in this genre on the strength of the
  word ตำนาน in one section title.
- **Start with the sixteen.** *Inference —* the Liap Lok witnesses are the highest-value
  target: one work, 16 copies, seven provinces, 1815–1933. A collation across them
  would show which stops on the Buddha's itinerary each muang chose to keep — which
  is a direct measurement of how the north edited its own charter.
- **The relic-chronicles are a gazetteer.** Site by site, the *that*-tamnan are
  primary sources for the history of the shrine and the polity around it. They
  should be walked against [the wats](/wats) and the [atlas](/atlas), not just
  [browsed](/browse?genre=tamnan_chronicle) as a list.
- **The chronicle/law seam is real and under-catalogued.** Two manuscripts titled
  *Tamnan Phanya Mangrai* are filed under *Law*, not here. Any full
  [Mangrai](/a?s=entity:mangrai) study has to read across both shelves at once.
- **Watch the sub-genre boundary.** The 54/31/15 split of
  [Buddhist Chronicle](/a?s=subgenre:tamnan_chronicle:Buddhist Chronicle) /
  [Tamnan](/a?s=subgenre:tamnan_chronicle:Tamnan) /
  [Secular History](/a?s=subgenre:tamnan_chronicle:Secular History) is a labelling
  artefact as much as a content one. Each sub-page says which parts of its own
  boundary it does not trust.
