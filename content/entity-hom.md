## What this is

Every other subject on this site is something the palm leaf keeps. This one is
something it does not. The blue shirt is the single most legible piece of Lanna
material culture in the world — it is what "northern Thai" *looks like* on a
poster — and across 6,986 catalogued manuscripts there is not one recipe for
making it. That absence is the page.

**ห้อม** *hom* /hôm/ is the plant: *Strobilanthes cusia* (Nees) Kuntze, of the
Acanthaceae, a shade-loving shrub of the northern hills, published for a century
under the synonym *Baphicacanthus cusia* and still sold under it. In the north
it is written **ฮ่อม**, and the two spellings are the *same sound*: ห is a
high-class consonant, so ห้อม with mai tho falls; ฮ is low-class, so ฮ่อม with
mai ek falls to the same place. Northern orthography reaches for ฮ as a matter
of habit — it is the letter kam mueang uses where central Thai has ร (รัก → ฮัก,
เรือน → เฮือน) — and the plant got carried along. It is not the lowland dye
plant. **คราม** *khram*
/khraam/ is *Indigofera tinctoria*, a legume of the plains and the northeast,
and the two are in different families, on different ground, worked by different
people. Thai prose uses คราม as the generic for the colour and the process; this
page follows the north and keeps ห้อม for the plant.

*Terms are given four ways where it helps: **script** — *RTGS* — /precise, with
tone and vowel length/ — then the root or the morpheme split. Tone: à low, á
high, â falling, ǎ rising, unmarked mid; doubled vowels are long.*

The garment is **หม้อห้อม** — หม้อ *mo* /mɔ̂ɔ/ "pot" + ห้อม — *the pot of
indigo*: a piece of clothing named after the vessel it was made in. Which is odd
enough that a rival etymology exists, and the state has had to rule on it.

### The spelling is a real argument, and the state has ruled on it

Signs in Phrae say **ม่อฮ่อม**. Signs in Bangkok say **หม้อห้อม**. Both are
pronounced identically in Bangkok Thai, and the fight over which is right is
about a hundred and fifty kilometres of vowel.

*Tradition holds —* the ม่อ camp derives the first syllable not from หม้อ "pot"
but from **มอ** *mo* /mɔɔ/, an old word for a dull dark colour — so ม่อฮ่อม would
mean "indigo-dark", a colour word, and the pot would be a folk etymology laid
over it afterwards.

The Office of the Royal Society has ruled against that. Its position is narrow
and worth stating exactly: ม่อฮ่อม should not be written, **not** because the
colour etymology is wrong, but because it is not a correct transposition of the
Payap (northern) sounds into Bangkok spelling. Bangkok Thai collapses ม่อฮ่อม and
หม้อฮ่อม into one pronunciation — both come out /mɔ̂ hɔ̂m/ — while northern speech
keeps them apart on the length of that first vowel: หม้อ the pot is long, ม่อ the
colour-word is short. The dictionary
entry that stands defines หม้อห้อม as *a round-necked, short-sleeved shirt,
opening down the front, usually dyed deep blue or black*, and notes ม่อห้อม and
ม่อฮ่อม as variants in use. The Royal Institute had already taken the question up
in a 1975 bulletin. *Inference —* a ruling that has to be re-issued for fifty
years is a ruling the signwriters are ignoring, and they are: the shops at Thung
Hong overwhelmingly paint ม่อฮ่อม.

### What the catalogue actually holds

Thirty-seven witnesses name cloth. Not one of them names a colour.

The distribution is the finding. Twenty-five are **canonical merit texts** — the
*anisong*, the reward-of-giving genre — and what they give is cloth: the kathin
robe, the rains-bathing cloth, the rag-robe, the banner. Five are filed under
**astrology**, two are chronicle, two ritual, three miscellaneous. And four
bundles — three of the astrological ones plus one the catalogue files as *other*
— are about *when you may cut cloth*. The scribes wrote about cloth constantly.
They wrote about giving it, about the day to cut it, about the merit that comes
back. They never once wrote down how to make it blue.

*Inference —* this is not an accident of survival. Palm leaf in Lanna is a
monastic technology: it is copied in a temple, stored in a temple, and its
subjects are the subjects a temple has a use for. Dyeing was women's work, done
in a yard, taught by hand, and it had no reason to enter that stream. The same
silence covers weaving, cooking and midwifery. The catalogue is not a record of
what Lanna knew. It is a record of what Lanna's monks copied.

## The blue is not in the plant

Cut a stem of ห้อม and it is green, and it stays green. There is no blue pigment
anywhere in it. What the leaf carries is **indican** — indoxyl bound to a sugar,
0.4–1.3% of the leaf by weight — which is colourless and water-soluble and does
nothing on its own.

The blue has to be manufactured in three steps, and traditional practice performs
all three without naming any of them:

1. **Hydrolysis.** Steep the cut stems under water for a night. A glucosidase in
   the plant splits indican into glucose and **indoxyl**. The liquor goes
   yellow-green and stinks. At Thung Hong this is a night and a turn — flip the
   bundle in the morning so the top leaves go under, and draw off in the
   afternoon: eighteen to twenty hours.
2. **Oxidation.** Strain the leaves out and beat lime into the liquor. Indoxyl
   pairs with itself in air and becomes **indigotin**, C₁₆H₁₀N₂O₂ — which is blue,
   and which is *insoluble in water*. It falls out as a paste, **เนื้อคราม**
   *nuea khram*, the colour of a bruise. Ban Khan Phalan burns cockle shell for
   its lime and reckons about ten grams to the litre.
3. **Reduction.** And now you have a problem, because a pigment that will not
   dissolve cannot dye anything. The paste must be *put back* to a soluble form —
   **leuco-indigo**, indigo-white, which is a yellow-green dianion — and held
   there, in an alkaline liquor, by bacteria that eat sugar and breathe the
   pigment. That liquor is the pot.

So the dyer dips cotton into a yellow-green bath and lifts out yellow-green
cloth, and it turns blue in her hands as the air gets at it. Every account of
indigo dyeing that has ever been written stops at that moment, because it is the
only step in the whole of textile practice that looks like magic and is not.

**ย้อมเย็น** *yom yen* — "cold dyeing" — is the trade's own term, and it exists
because indigo is the exception. Every other natural dye in the northern
repertoire is extracted by boiling and taken up hot. Indigo cannot be: heat does
not dissolve it, and the vat's bacteria die. It is the one dye you must keep at
blood heat and no hotter, which is why the pot stands raised off the ground where
morning and evening sun can reach it.

## The pot is alive, and it is named for a colour monks may not wear

This is the part that belongs to this site rather than to a chemistry book.

A reduction vat is a microbial culture. It has a population, an appetite, a
metabolism and a death, and the vocabulary that grew up around it in Thai is not
metaphorical language for a chemical process — it is the language you would use
for an animal, applied because nothing else fits.

In Isan the vessel is **หม้อนิล** *mo nin* /mɔ̂ɔ nin/, and building one is
**ก่อหม้อนิล** *ko mo nin* — to *found* a pot, the verb used for laying a fire or
raising a wall. *Tradition holds —* the pot is inhabited: a spirit lives in it,
and the practice around it is care, not procedure. The recorded vocabulary of its
states, gathered from dyers in Ubon Ratchathani in 2006, reads:

- **หม้อนิลหิว** — *the pot is hungry.* The liquor has gone yellow and stirring
  raises no foam. Feed it lye or lose it.
- **หม้อนิลลักหนี** — *the pot has stolen away.* The spirit in it has gone; the
  liquor spoils within days and will not dye.
- **หม้อนิลเป็น** — *the pot is alive.* A blue foam-cap sits on the surface and
  will not collapse when you touch it. The villagers call that cap **โพนปลวก**
  *phon pluak*, "the termite mound". Beneath it the liquor is dark green and the
  sediment is blue. This is the pot you can dye in.
- **หม้อนิลตาย** — *the pot is dead.* Throw the indigo out and found a new one.

And a pot that has run can come back: a *lak-ni* pot that recovers is said to
have become *pen* again, which is the same word Thai uses for a person who
survives.

The feeding is literal. Wood-ash lye — **น้ำด่าง** *nam dang*, filtered a dipper
at a time through ash tied in cloth, and **tasted**, because the old rule is that
lye which is not salty is thrown away and never used. Half a ripe **กล้วยน้ำว้า**
banana. Small pinched balls of tamarind. A ladle of paste. A dipper of liquor
begged from a pot that is already alive, as a starter — the word used is
**เชื้อ** *chuea*, the same word as a yeast culture, or an infection, or a lineage.
Three or four times a day the dyer performs **โจกคราม** *chok khram*: lift the
liquor and pour it back through the air. Then, once a day, about a pickled-vegetable
tin of lye, so the pot does not die.

The instrument reading is exact. A new vat runs at pH 12.5–13 and drifts down to
10–10.5 over roughly twenty days, and the whole apparatus of banana and tamarind
and ash and daily pouring is an unwritten titration performed by tongue and eye.
One pot yields one dip a day. A dark shade wants many, so a serious dyer keeps
several pots, and four or five days of dip-and-cure. After each dip the yarn is
wrung and left overnight in a covered vessel to cure before it is rinsed: dipped
in the morning, cured till evening; dipped in the evening, cured till morning.

### นิล

**นิล** *nin* /nin/ is Sanskrit and Pali **nīla** — dark blue, blue-black; the
same syllable that gives Thai the black gem. It is also the exact word in which
the Vinaya forbids a colour to a monk.

The Mahāvagga allows six classes of dye and no others — root, stem, bark, leaf,
flower, fruit ([Mv.VIII.10.1](https://www.dhammatalks.org/vinaya/bmc/Section0041.html)) —
and then rules that robes *entirely nīla* may not be worn, along with those
entirely yellow, blood-red, crimson, black, orange or beige
([Mv.VIII.29](https://www.dhammatalks.org/vinaya/bmc/Section0041.html)). The
commentarial discussion, when it comes to leaf-dyes, singles indigo out. What is
left after the exclusions is the dull red-brown of bark, and that is the robe.

*Inference —* two things follow, and the second is the one worth having. The
first is trivial: indigo is a leaf-dye, formally allowed by the six-fold rule and
knocked out by the colour rule. The second is that a lay village and a monastery
standing fifty metres apart, dyeing cloth in the same water with the same plants,
arrived at *opposite* colours by the same text — and the peasant's pot is named,
in the dialect that has least Pali in it, with the Pali word for the colour a monk
may not wear. The blue shirt is not indifferent to the robe. It is the robe's
complement, and it is spelled that way.

## The colour belongs to Saturn

The Vinaya explains why a monk cannot wear it. It does not explain why a
farmer *wants* to — and the answer to that is not comfort, and not fashion.
It is planetary.

**นิล** is not only a colour word. In the **นพรัตน์** *nopparat* — the nine
gems, *nava-ratna*, which are the nine planets worn as stones — the dark blue
stone is **นิลกาฬ** *ninlakan* (← *nīla* + *kāḷa*, blue + black), the blue
sapphire, and it is **พระเสาร์**'s. Saturn. The nine-gem ring, the นพเก้า, is
the single most-worn auspicious object in Thailand and every stone in it is a
planet; the indigo one is the planet the tradition treats most carefully.

*Tradition holds —* Saturn's own deva is described as dark-bodied and adorned
with ไพลิน and นิลรัตน์ — sapphire and nil-stones. He wears the colour. And the
prescription for his day is stated in the same Indic vocabulary the Vinaya
uses: Saturday's ornament is **กัณหาภรณ์** *kanhaphon* ← Pali *kaṇha*
"black" + *ābharaṇa* "ornament".

Which closes a circle worth stopping on. *kaṇha* is on the Mahāvagga's list of
robe colours a monk may not wear, one line from *nīla*. The astrologer prescribes
for Saturday exactly what the Vinaya forbids to the robe — and the whole week
agrees with the astrologer: the everyday scheme gives Saturday **ม่วง**, purple,
and the campaign list in *Sawatdirak*, which the manual in this collection
reprints, gives Saturday **ดำ**, black
([Compendium of Katha from Old Manuals](/m/compendium-katha-from-old-manuals-6966/),
pp. 15–16; [magic & ritual](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual)). Two lists for two
occasions, both putting the dark end of the spectrum on Saturn's day.

*Inference —* so the blue is not neutral and it is not merely practical. It is a
planetary colour in a system where colours belong to planets, worn by people who
know that. And when the Isan dyer names her vat **หม้อนิล** she is
using the gem's name — the pot is called after Saturn's stone. That may be
nothing more than the ordinary Thai word for blue-black doing ordinary work.
It may also be why a pot has a spirit in it at all, when a rice pot does not.
The corpus cannot settle that; it is recorded here as the question.

One witness ties the two ends together on a single folio. The Phayao bundle
[Sado kho, Tam khwan cao nai … Wen tan pha lwat](/m/sado-kho-tam-khwan-cao-nai-thwai-dok-mai-tian-nam-op-nam-hom-5524/)
at [Wat Si Khom Kham](/place/wat-si-khom-kham-phayao/) holds
**สะเดาะเคราะห์** — the rite that undoes a bad turn in the chart — the
soul-calling for a lord, the New Year offering of scented waters, and
**เวนทานผ้าลวาด**, the giving of the *pha lwat* cloth. Fate-remedy and
cloth-gift, copied by one hand into one bundle. It is also one of the
thirty-seven.

## What it actually protects against

Ask what indigo wards off and the tradition answers in three different
registers, and they do not agree with each other. That disagreement is the
interesting part, so they are kept apart here.

**The empirical one is real and is not folklore.** Indigo's own molecules —
tryptanthrin and isatin, which ride into the cloth with the pigment — are
antibacterial, antifungal and anti-inflammatory; the dye smells wrong to
insects; and indigo cloth is measurably good against ultraviolet. Every
tradition that works the pot has found this out independently. *Tradition holds
—* the Lanna and Isan version is a farmer's: an indigo shirt is why a man
working sun-up to sun-down in open paddy does not burn, and the cool paste
itself, **เนื้อคราม**, goes onto a centipede or scorpion sting to draw the
poison and take the pain down. Japan reached the same place from the other
side — samurai wore indigo under armour against wound infection, and the fire
brigades wore it for the way it resists a flame. A cloth that keeps out sun,
insects, rot and infection is a protective garment before anyone says a word
over it.

**The hill-country one is explicit.** *Tradition holds —* among the Hmong, and
in varying form among the Akha, Mien and Lisu who work the same plant on the
same slopes, indigo is a ward. The batik and the embroidery laid over it are
built as a field that turns evil aside, and the most heavily worked pieces in
the whole repertoire are the ones made for **infants** — the baby carrier and
the baby cap, the two things a spirit would come for. Some caps carry eyes,
to be looked back at. *Inference —* the logic is the oldest one there is for a
dark colour: what cannot be picked out cannot be taken, and a child dressed
the colour of dusk is a child a spirit walks past. This is not a Lanna
tradition and this page does not claim it as one — but the ห้อม on those
slopes and the ห้อม in the Phrae pot is the same plant, cut on the same hills.

**And the Thai amulet tradition says no.** This is the corrective, and it
matters more than the two claims above. If indigo were the protective colour of
central Thai magic, the protective cloth would be indigo. It is not.
**ผ้าประเจียด** *pha prachiat* — the inscribed cloth tied round the head or the
upper arm, the ancestor of the muay thai armband — is cut from **ผ้าสาลู**,
plain white, and worked in white, red and yellow. The yantra cloth is white
because a yantra has to be *read*; ink on indigo is ink on nothing
([yantra](/a?s=entity:yantra)). So the drawn magic and the dyed magic are two
different technologies with two different colour rules, and the corpus's 37
cloth witnesses sit almost entirely on the drawn side — which is one more way
of saying the same thing this page has said throughout. The leaf kept the
side of cloth that could be written on.

## What the leaf keeps instead: the day you are allowed to cut cloth

These four are the ones nobody expects, and they are the argument for reading a
catalogue rather than searching it. Three sit under *astrology* and one under
*other*, which is itself worth noticing: the classifier could not decide whether
a cloth-cutting day was a fortune-telling text or a miscellany, and that
hesitation is the right one.

**ตัดเสื้อผ้า** *tat suea pha* — "cutting clothes" — is an auspicious-day
question, and four Lanna bundles hold the rule:
[Tat sue pha, Liang phi, Pi ao phua ao mia](/m/tat-sue-pha-liang-phi-pi-ao-phua-ao-mia-lae-uen-uen-5128/) at
Wat Wiang in Lampang, which files the cloth-cutting day
beside the day to feed the spirits and the year to take a husband or a wife;
an untitled Lampang bundle from Wat Ban Luk that runs
[*wan tat suea pha, wan nung pha, wan mai, wan lokawinat*](/m/wan-tat-suea-pha-wan-nung-pha-wan-mai-wan-lokawinat-wan-mae-5469/)
— the day to cut cloth, the day to put it on, new year's day, and the day the
world is destroyed, in that order; and a Phayao compendium at
[Wat Si Khom Kham](/place/wat-si-khom-kham-phayao/) that
[sets it after the day to first eat the rice and the naming of oxen and buffalo](/m/wan-haek-khao-cue-ngua-khwai-phi-hua-luang-wan-tat-suea-pha-5497/).

The fourth is the one to read twice.
[Tat suea pha, Ngua, Hung, Maeo, Suea, Latcasi, Nak, Cang, Nu](/m/tat-suea-pha-ngua-hung-maeo-suea-latcasi-nak-cang-nu-lae-uen-5131/),
Wat Wiang, Lampang: *cutting clothes; ox; rainbow; cat; tiger; lion; otter;
elephant; rat.* A garment sits in a list of omens — one row among the animals
whose appearance means something. *Inference —* this is the register the leaf
puts cloth in. Not craft, not commerce: **reading**. Cloth is a thing that can be
timed wrongly, like a marriage or a housepost, and the manuscript that knows this
is the same manuscript that knows what a rainbow means. The elephant is in that
list too, one item along ([the elephant](/a?s=entity:chang)) — the same folio, the
same eye.

The merit texts hold the other half. Ten witnesses across five provinces carry
**ผ้าบังสุกุล** *pha bangsukun* ← Pali *paṃsukūla*, *paṃsu* "dust" + *kūla*
"heap" — the rag-robe, cloth taken from a dust-heap or a corpse and made into a
robe, the most ascetic garment the tradition has and the one every Lanna scribe
seems to have copied the merit of: [Wat Phra Sing](/place/wat-phra-sing/)
in Chiang Mai, Wat Lao Noi in Lampang,
[Wat Si Khom Kham](/place/wat-si-khom-kham-phayao/) in Phayao,
Wat Phothanaram in Chiang Rai, and a Lamphun copy.
Beside them the kathin robe, the rains-bathing cloth, the banner-cloth on its
pole, and the mouth-and-head wiping cloths offered with the monastery buildings.

And the two witnesses that contain the word **ย้อม** *yom* /yɔ́ɔm/, "to dye", are
neither of them about dyeing cloth:
[Katha long nyan pha sahang lae nyom kham hue ngam](/m/katha-long-nyan-pha-sahang-lae-nyom-kham-hue-ngam-360/)
at the Nan Provincial Museum is a spell for laying yantra on cloth, bundled with
one for *ย้อมฅำหื้องาม* — making gold beautiful, the Lanna ฅ still standing in the
title; and a [Wat Chiang Man](/place/wat-chiang-man/) bundle whose second title
the catalogue reads as [*Sut nyom simma*](/m/inthanin-singkaha-sut-nyom-simma-6084/), a formula
touching the ordination boundary. The corpus's only two dye-words are about gold
and about ground.

*Inference —* the counter on this page will move if the crawl ever reaches a
household manual rather than a monastery shelf, and the aliases are already set
to catch it. Until then, 37 is not a small number for cloth. It is a complete
number for cloth-as-the-temple-saw-it, and zero is the complete number for colour.

## Why Friday

Ask in Chiang Mai and you get told, correctly, that people wear mo hom on
Fridays. The reason is not old, and it is not northern, and it is written down.

**Cabinet resolution of 9 June 2020** (BE 2563) approved measures to promote
Thai cloth, of which the first asks officials and the public to dress in Thai
or local cloth **at least two days a week — on Tuesday and Friday**. The
Ministry of the Interior pressed provincial governors to drive it; ministries
wrote their own versions; the guidance to a department is to declare a
Thai-dress day on a Friday or a Tuesday, or more often. What each province
wears is left to the province, so the policy is one and the garments are many:
the north puts on mo hom, and **Sakon Nakhon puts on ผ้าย้อมคราม, its own
indigo, from the other plant** — the northeast keeps the same Friday with
*Indigofera* where the north keeps it with *Strobilanthes*. Mukdahan wears
Mukdahan cloth. The rule is national; the blue is local twice over.

That is the whole documented answer, and the habit is older than the
resolution in practice — provincial and departmental Friday dress days ran for
years before 2020, which is what the resolution was tidying up. This page has
found no earlier instrument that fixes the day, and says so rather than
inventing one.

*Inference —* but the day is not arbitrary either, and here the two halves of
this page meet. In the **สีประจำวัน** system — the auspicious colour of each
weekday, which Thai tradition derives from the body-colours of the
**เทวดานพเคราะห์**, the nine planetary devas — **Friday is สีฟ้า, blue**, from
พระศุกร์, Venus. So a northerner in an indigo shirt on a Friday is doing two
unrelated things at once: complying with a 2020 administrative measure, and
wearing the day's own colour. Nobody had to arrange that. It is the kind of
coincidence a living tradition absorbs and then explains, and within a
generation the second reason will be the one people give.

Two cautions on that. First, the state's reasoning is documented and it is about
craft income and community markets, not about Venus; the alignment is real but it
is not a cause.

Second — and this one is a correction to an earlier version of this page, which
got it wrong and was caught by a reader who wears these colours. **Do not reach
for the manual's list here.** The wardrobe rule in
[Compendium of Katha from Old Manuals](/m/compendium-katha-from-old-manuals-6966/)
(pp. 15–16) is *Sawatdirak*, and *Sawatdirak* opens the passage
**อนึ่งภูษาผ้าทรงณรงค์รบ — ให้มีครบเครื่องเสร็จทั้งเจ็ดสี**: "as for the cloth worn
*going into battle*, have all seven colours complete." It is **campaign dress**,
worn on the day you fight, applying whatever day you were born on. Its seven are
red, white, purple-with-indigo, orange-red, yellow-green, cloud-grey, black.

The everyday scheme is the other list, and it is the one that governs what anyone
puts on for a Friday: Sunday red, Monday yellow, **Tuesday pink**, Wednesday
green, **Thursday orange**, Friday blue, **Saturday purple**. That is what people
wear and what they will tell you is traditional, and they are right.

*Inference —* setting those two lists side by side and calling the difference
historical drift is comparing a helmet with a shirt. Both trace themselves to the
same **มหาทักษา** root — the body-colours of the planetary devas — and they land
differently because they are for different occasions. Which came first is not
something this page can settle, and it no longer pretends to. What survives, and
is worth keeping, is narrower and firmer: on **both** lists the dark end of the
week belongs to Saturn — black in the campaign list, purple in the everyday one —
and indigo appears in the campaign list on Tuesday, as war-cloth. A practitioner
following *Sawatdirak* wore indigo to fight in. He did not wear it on Tuesdays for
luck.

## The village is named for an anvil

**บ้านทุ่งโฮ้ง** *Ban Thung Hong*, Tambon Thung Hong, Amphoe Mueang, Phrae —
18.174 N, 100.166 E, five *muban*, about 4,840 people in 2,037 households — is
where the world buys the shirt. It is a **Tai Phuan** (ไทพวน) village. Its people
came from Muang Phuan in Xieng Khouang, in what is now Laos, moved south by the
Siamese redistribution of Phuan populations among the armies that had fought in
the Lao campaigns: seventeen families into Nan in 1834 (CS 1196 / BE 2377), then
relatives after them, over decades.

*Tradition holds —* the name has nothing to do with dye. It is **ทั่ง** *thang*
/thâŋ/, an anvil — a place of hammering — plus **โห้ง** *hong* /hôoŋ/, Phuan for a
deep hollow: *Ban Thang Hong*, the smithy in the basin, worn down to Thung Hong.
The village that clothes the north in blue is named after iron, and its oldest
recorded taboo is a smith's: the forge spirit **ผีเตาเหล็ก** had to be propitiated
annually or it turned into a **ผีกะ**, so households now raise a temporary forge
for a job and pull it down after, to give the spirit nothing to settle in.

They also keep **กำฟ้า** *kam fa* — the sky rite, January or February, three to
five days in which no work is done at all, listening for the direction of the
first thunder and reading next season's rain out of it. *Inference —* a Phuan
village keeping a Phuan sky-rite two centuries after the march is the same
mechanism that kept the pot: displaced people hold their technique harder than
settled ones, because it is the part of home that travels.

Since 6 August 2005 the natural-dye work at Thung Hong has been organised as
**วิสาหกิจชุมชนหม้อห้อมทุ่งเจริญย้อมสีธรรมชาติ**, a registered community
enterprise; the cloth has been an OTOP handicraft-tourism village; and on
**30 March 2020** *ผ้าหม้อห้อมแพร่* was registered as a Thai geographical
indication, which fixes in law what had been a claim — that the name belongs to
Phrae, and to indigo grown there.

## The costume was invented in Chiang Mai, in 1953

The shirt is old. The *uniform* is not, and it has a date and an address.

*Tradition holds —* in 1953 (BE 2496) **ไกรศรี นิมมานเหมินท์** Kraisri
Nimmanhaeminda, then the northern regional director of a Bangkok bank and the man
who turned a household meal into a public form, gave a **ขันโตก** khantoke dinner
in Chiang Mai in honour of Sanya Dharmasakti and the American consul — and set a
dress code: a round-necked cotton shirt, indigo-dyed, with a **ผ้าขาวม้า**
*phakhaoma* at the waist. It was copied, and copied, and it is now what a northern
man wears to a formal northern occasion.

*Inference —* three consequences, all visible today. The shirt entered polite
society as *costume* rather than as clothing, which is why it is worn to festivals
and Fridays and not to work. It was formalised in Chiang Mai and manufactured in
Phrae, which is why the province that made it famous is not the province that
sells the most of it. And it was chosen by a man who was cataloguing Lanna at the
same time — Kraisri is a founding figure of northern Thai studies — so the garment
arrived already framed as heritage, by someone qualified to frame it. The road in
Chiang Mai that carries his family's name is the one the boutiques are on.

That framing narrowed the thing. The reference works are plainer: an indigo
round-necked shirt is documented for **Tai Lue** of Sipsongpanna, for **Lao** in
Laos and the Thai northeast, and for **Tai Yuan** in the north. It was, before it
was Phrae's, simply what Tai people wore to work in.

## Two chemistries, and the fade that saved it

Adolf von Baeyer cracked indigo's structure in **1878** and spent nineteen years
and more than BASF's stated capital getting to a process that paid. Synthetic
indigo went on sale in **1897**, into such disbelief that BASF hired an outside
firm to certify it. Within a decade about ninety-five per cent of the natural
indigo trade was gone; a world crop of roughly nineteen thousand tonnes was under
a thousand by 1914. Baeyer took the Nobel in 1905.

What survived that was not the industry. It was the pot — in exactly the places
where nobody was selling into the world market, which is to say northern villages
dyeing for themselves. *Inference —* the reason Thung Hong still has the practice
is that in 1897 it was not worth destroying.

And the test that separates the two is beautifully backwards. **Real
indigo-dyed cloth crocks.** It runs in the first washes; it fades; it wants
re-dyeing; the tradition's own advice is to wash it out several times and expect
the water to run blue, and to accept that the garment will get *better* looking as
it goes. The synthetic dye does not do this — its colour holds. So the flaw is the
proof, and a mo hom shirt that never bleeds is a printed one. This is the single
most useful fact on this page for anyone standing in a shop, and it is worked
through, with prices and the walk, on the living-city side at
[motdang.net/hom.html](https://motdang.net/hom.html).

## The plant is also a drug

ห้อม is not only a dye, and its medical career is older and better documented
than its textile one.

The root and rhizome of *Strobilanthes cusia* are **南板藍根** *nán bǎn lán gēn*,
"southern ban-lan-gen", a Chinese materia medica with about a thousand years of
use — for fevers, swelling, inflammation, and in the modern pharmacopoeia for
viral complaints. The leaf and stem are *Malanye*, southern *daqingye*, an
antipyretic. And the processed pigment itself is **青黛** *qīngdài*, indigo
naturalis, applied to skin disease. In northern Thailand the **Mien (Yao)** use
the leaves against diarrhoea.

The village pharmacology is the same knowledge from the other end. Dyers say the
indigo in the cloth is why a farmer working sun-up to sun-down does not burn, and
that the cool paste is what you put on a centipede or scorpion sting to draw the
poison and take the pain down. *Inference —* a plant that is simultaneously a
textile, a fever medicine and a poultice is exactly the profile the
[medicine manuscripts](/a?s=genre:medicine) record for a dozen other species, and
its absence from them is a gap in the catalogue rather than in the tradition.

## Where the word is on the ground

The living-city directory for Chiang Mai and Chiang Rai held 158 craft and
tailoring shops on 31 August 2026 and **not one of them had ห้อม in its name** — the trade is real and the
signage is not, because the shirt is sold as a shirt. What the map does keep is
the plant. Three watercourses are named for it — **ห้วยห้อม** *Huai Hom* in Mae
Ngon, Fang (Chiang Mai) and again in Pa Tan, Khun Tan (Chiang Rai); **ห้วยฮ่อม**
in Ban Chan, Galyani Vadhana — and a hamlet, **บ้านเด่นฮ่อม** in Bo Kaeo, Samoeng — four in all, and the sibling counts them
live, so that number is the one to trust.
*Inference —* Northern place-names take the form ห้วย + *plant*, and a creek
called Indigo Creek in three separate valleys is where the plant grew thickly
enough to name the water. Guard the reading, though: ฮ่อม in kam mueang is also an
ordinary word for a lane.

The drawn version of everything on this page — the migration route from Xieng
Khouang, the chemistry as a cycle, the pot's four states as a diagnostic dial, and
the timeline from 1834 to the GI — is at
[motdang.net/hom.html](https://motdang.net/hom.html). This page keeps the
tradition; that one keeps the walk.
