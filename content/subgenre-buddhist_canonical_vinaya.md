## What this is

The **discipline basket** — the *Vinayapiṭaka*, the code by which the saṅgha
governs itself — and the second-largest canonical sub-genre at **494 witnesses**.

It is the most misread heading in the archive. "Monastic rules" sounds like an
abstraction. The Vinaya is the opposite of an abstraction: it is **case law**, and
every clause in it was written down in response to an incident. The canonical form
is fixed — *at one time a monk did X; people complained; the Buddha convened the
saṅgha and laid down the rule* — so the text of the discipline is also, unavoidably,
a chronicle of everything monks got up to in the first century of the order. The
Lanna copies keep that shape, and the working documents built on top of them keep
the incidents fossilised in the questions they ask.

## Every rule has a defendant

Watch it happen in the ordination script. Before a candidate can be admitted, the
examining teacher takes him aside and asks a fixed list of questions — reproduced
in full in the contributed ritual manual
[Conducting Religious Rituals Complete](/m/conducting-religious-rituals-complete-6971/)
(p.26–27), where the answers are printed alongside so nobody improvises. Five
questions he must answer *no* to:

- Leprosy? Boils? Skin disease? Consumption? Epilepsy?

And eight he must answer *yes* to:

- Are you a human being? Are you a man? Are you a free man, not a bonded servant?
  Are you free of debt? Are you not in the king's service? Have you your mother's
  and father's permission? Are you fully twenty years of age? Are your bowl and
  robes complete?

Read that list as what it is: **a list of things that went wrong once.** *Tradition
holds —* the debt question is there because creditors came to the monastery looking
for a man who had escaped into the robe; the king's-service question because
deserting soldiers did the same; the parents' question because a boy called
Rāhula's grandfather asked the Buddha to stop taking sons without asking; the
twenty-years question because novices ordained too young sickened and died in the
cold. And the first question — *are you a human being?* — is there, tradition holds,
because a [nāga](/a?s=entity:naga) once took the form of a young man and ordained,
and was discovered only when he fell asleep and reverted to his serpent shape in the
monks' quarters. He was sent away weeping, and the Buddha allowed that a candidate
should henceforth be called a *nāga* on the day of his ordination, in memory of him.
The word is still used. Two and a half thousand years later, at a wat in Chiang Mai,
the boy in the white robe on the morning of his ordination is called a naga.

Then the bowl is hung on his body and the four garments are pointed out one at a
time — *this is your bowl; this is the outer robe; this is the upper robe; this is
the inner robe* — and he answers "Yes, Venerable Sir" four times, because the
Vinaya requires that a monk be shown to *have* them before he can be one.

The archive holds this liturgy in its own hand as well: **26 witnesses in this
basket** carry *kammavācā* (กัมมวาจา) in the title — the formal act of the saṅgha,
the exact words by which a novice becomes a monk. The oldest dated is
[หนังสือกัมมวาจา](/m/nangsue-kammavaca-2-1264/) at Wat Kittiwong in Mae Hong Son,
**1724** — nine folios, which is all the words it takes.

And on one of them you can read the questionnaire itself.
[Ms 5507](/m/kammawaca-sut-thon-pha-lae-bat-5507/) at Wat Si Khom Kham in Phayao is
the **only Vinaya manuscript in this archive with its pages transcribed**, and pages
4–5 carry the examination in Pali, question and answer alternating down the leaf:
*gaṇḍo?* — **natthi bhante**; *kilāso?* — **natthi bhante**; *apamāro?* — **natthi
bhante**; then the turn to the affirmatives, **āma bhante**. Boils, no venerable
sir. Skin disease, no venerable sir. Epilepsy, no venerable sir. Page 11 has the
opening of the motion itself — *suṇātu me bhante saṅgho*, "let the Sangha hear me" —
and page 14 the confession formula, *sambahulā āpattiyo*, "many offences." The
printed manual's Thai table and this mulberry-paper folding book are the same
document, in different centuries and different hands. And this is the same bundle
that ends with a [thon](/a?s=entity:sut_thon) rite for the robe and the bowl — see
below.

## The ladder of offences

The Lanna shelf holds the code by its parts, not as a whole book, and *tradition
holds* those parts are graded by severity — the counts below are this basket's:

- **ปาราชิก / pārājika**, the four defeats that end monkhood permanently — 30
  witnesses in this basket, including a
  [*Sap palacikkan*](/m/sap-palacikkan-269/) word-gloss at Wat Don Chai Phra Bat in
  Nan, 1895, running to 381 folios in 21 fascicles. A word-list on the four
  unforgivable offences, longer than most whole texts here.
- **สังฆาทิเสส / saṅghādisesa**, the offences needing formal saṅgha action.
- **ปาจิตตีย์ / pācittiya**, the ninety-two requiring expiation — 30 witnesses.
- **ปาฏิโมกข์ / pātimokkha**, the fortnightly recitation of the whole rule-list —
  20 witnesses.

The contributed teaching manual
[Practicing Kammatthana vol. 3](/m/practicing-kammatthana-vol-3-luang-por-ruesi-ling-dam-6963/)
(p.99) puts the ladder in a preacher's mouth and then knocks it down: lay people
keep five precepts or eight, novices ten, monks two hundred and twenty-seven — *and
even the 227 are not the whole of it*, because there is higher conduct beyond them.
Then the warning, which is really a portrait of how discipline actually erodes:
those who study only the scriptural side teach that a *dukkaṭa* offence doesn't
matter, and a *pācittiya* doesn't matter, it's a small thing, and a *nissaggiya*
doesn't matter either, and when it gets heavier a *saṅghādisesa* doesn't matter, and
finally even a *pārājika* doesn't matter. "If small losses are let go, big losses
will follow too, because the heart becomes numb and unruly." The same manual sends
an unsure monk to sit down with the *Navakovāda* and read it regularly. The
discipline is a thing you can lose by inches.

## The way back

What makes the Vinaya law rather than exhortation is that it has a **procedure for
rehabilitation**, and the north copied that procedure carefully. A monk who has
fallen into a saṅghādisesa offence and concealed it undergoes **ปริวาส *parivāsa***
(probation for as long as he concealed it), then **มานัตต *mānatta*** (six nights of
penance), then **อัพภาน *abbhāna*** (formal restoration by a chapter of twenty).

That three-step sequence appears across the canonical genre as a named object over
and over — sometimes filed as Vinaya, sometimes not:

- [กัมมวาจา, กฐิน, สิมมา, ปริวาร, มานัตต, อัพพาน](/m/kammawaca-kathin-simma-paliwan-manat-appan-2525/)
  at [Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/) — the complete formal-acts kit in one
  bundle: ordination words, kathin, boundary, probation, penance, restoration.
- *Sut paliwasakam manatakam appanakam* at Wat Hia in Nan, 1932; *Kho wat manat,
  Kho wat aphan* at Wat Si Khom Kham, 1924; *Withi khao paliwat* — "the method of
  entering probation" — at Wat Yuan, 1927; a 1965 *Khao paliwasakam* in verse at
  Wat Ban Ueam.
- And [อัพพานสุททันตะปริวาส](/m/abbhana-suddanta-parivasa-1873/) at Wat Sung Men,
  dated **1965** — the second-newest dated witness in the basket, behind only a
  [Pātimokkha of 1973](/m/patimok-kahat-4328/). *Inference —* of everything in this
  basket, one of the last two things still being written out by hand in living
  memory is the procedure by which a monk who has failed comes back.

## Drawing the line

Nothing in the saṅgha's formal life is valid outside a properly determined
**สีมา *sīmā***, the consecrated boundary. Get the boundary wrong and every
ordination performed inside it is void — which is why *Sīmā-vinicchaya*, "the
adjudication of boundaries," is copied as a text in its own right:
[at Wat Lom Raed in Lampang in 1740](/m/sima-winitsai-3823/), and at Wat Sung Men
[bound together with the probation procedure](/m/sima-winitsai-paliwasakam-1870/) in
1840. The stakes are visible in a companion text on the
[Anisong shelf](/browse?subgenre=Anisong):
[**อานิสงขุดสิม**](/m/anisong-khut-sim-2674/) at Wat Phra Bat Ming Mueang in Phrae,
the merit of *digging* the sīmā — the boundary is a hole a layman pays to have dug
and a legal instrument, at the same time.

## The robe that has to be finished before dawn

The most spectacular piece of Vinaya procedure in the corpus is the **จุลกฐิน,
Chula Kathin** — the "running kathin" — set out step by step in
[Setting Up Ceremonies, Ancient Edition](/m/setting-up-ceremonies-ancient-edition-6969/)
(p.95–96). The kathin robe must be offered within the month after the Rains
Retreat; the *chula* form insists the cloth also be **made from scratch inside a
single day**.

So the sponsor and his helpers start from raw cotton still in the boll, and perform
a rite over it treating it as though it were being sown, sprouting, growing,
flowering, forming bolls and ripening — then pick it, gin out the seeds, card it,
spin thread, braid it into skeins, reel it, size it with rice water, dry it, wind
the bobbins, thread the shuttle and weave the cloth, in a quantity sufficient for a
robe. It goes to the monk appointed robe-keeper, who processes it by the discipline.
Then the sponsor is back at work: kneading, pounding, washing, drying, cutting,
sewing, dyeing, drying again, folding, pressing, and offering it a second time for
the formal marking and determination. Only then are the monks convened to be told,
and to rejoice. And the whole thing "must be finished before the dawn of the new
day; otherwise, that kathin does not count as a valid kathin."

The item list for a royally-bestowed kathin set in the same manual (p.112) is a
quiet delight of the same discipline meeting the twentieth century: cloth for the
principal Buddha image, **a bundle of pātimokkha candles**, a triple robe, an alms
bowl with its bag — and a 2.5-litre electric water flask, and a boxed set of
screwdriver, hammer and pliers. The archive holds the merit-sermon for the older
half of that list: **fourteen canonical witnesses** name the kathin, the earliest
[อานิสงหื้ผ้ากถินเปันทาน](/m/anisong-hue-pha-kathin-pen-tan-1254/) at Wat Kittiwong
in Mae Hong Son, **1667** — a single fascicle of fourteen folios on why a robe is
worth all that.

## Illuminated at the Burmese edge

Along the western rim the basket changes appearance entirely. At **Wat Muai To** in
Mae Hong Son sit [five Kammawa](/m/kammawa-179/) — ก่ำมะหว่า — in **Burmese script**,
Pali-and-Burmese, on palm-leaf, of twelve to twenty-eight folios each, and **all five
are catalogued as illustrated**. Three more sit at Wat Kam Ko nearby. *Tradition
holds —* the Burmese *kammavācā* is a genre of luxury object as much as a text: short,
lacquered, gilded, sometimes written in thick tamarind-seed script on cloth stiffened
with lacquer, commissioned by a family as the merit-gift that accompanies a son into
the robe. **22 of the 494 are recorded as illustrated** — the highest share of any
canonical basket, against ten in the sutta and eight in the Abhidhamma — and this
handful of ordination-texts from one Mae Hong Son temple is a good part of them. *Inference —* the one part of the discipline basket anybody thought worth
decorating is the part read aloud at the moment a family gives away a son.

## The shape of the collection

Physically canonical: **461 of 494 on palm-leaf** (93%), 29 on mulberry paper, one
on khoi. Its language tilt is the sharpest evidence of what it is for — **199
monolingual Pali against 272 Pali-and-Lan Na**. The formal legal and liturgical
Pali needs no gloss, because it is not being understood, it is being *performed
exactly*. What does get glossed is the vocabulary: **91 of the 494 titles are
ศัพท์ / นามศัพท์ word-lists**, far more than in any other basket — a Vinaya
practitioner needs to know precisely what each technical term covers, because that
is where cases turn.

378 are dated, spanning **1537 to 1973**. The oldest is
[ฏีกากังขวิตรณี](/m/tika-kankhavitarani-3-4527/), the sub-commentary on the
*Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī* Pātimokkha commentary, at
[Wat Lai Hin Luang](/place/wat-lai-hin-luang/) in Lampang, 1537 — again the
commentary before the code. The largest is a second bundle of the
[*Samantapāsādika*](/m/samanta-mat-song-865/) at Wat Sung Men, 1839: **859 folios in
21 fascicles**, the heaviest single object in the basket. Like the
[sutta](/a?s=subgenre:buddhist_canonical:Sutta) it concentrates at
[Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/) (199, 40%), then
[Wat Lai Hin Luang](/place/wat-lai-hin-luang/) (42), Wat Ban Luk (29),
[Wat Phra That Lampang Luang](/place/wat-phra-that-lampang-luang/) and
[Wat Duang Di](/place/wat-duang-di/) (21 each). Script: 406 Tham Lanna, 74 Tham Lao,
nine Burmese, three Shan, two Khom.

## Where the discipline gets its hands dirty

A rule-book is a clean object; a working monastery is not, and the bundles show it.

- [วิไนยคุรุปเทส, อุโบสถ, ปวารณา, แปลอาบัติ](/m/winai-kulupatet-ubosot-pawalana-pae-abat-5050/)
  at Wat Ban Ueam, Lampang, 1889 — the teacher's Vinaya notes, the fortnightly
  uposatha, the **pavāraṇā** (the ceremony at the end of the Rains in which every
  monk formally *invites* the others to tell him what they saw him do wrong), and a
  translation of the offences. The whole disciplinary year in one hand.
- [ภิกขุฝากของแก่กัน, สูดกัมมะวาจา](/m/phikkhu-fak-khong-kae-kan-sut-kammawaca-2866/)
  at [Wat Lai Hin Luang](/place/wat-lai-hin-luang/) — "monks entrusting things to
  one another," bound with the kammavācā. Property law, monastic edition.
- *Kam sia abat* (คำเสียอาบัต) at Wat Hia, Nan — the actual words for confessing an
  offence, filed next to a meditation manual.
- And the seam that earns this basket its one research-priority flag:
  [กัมมวาจา, สูตถอนผ้า และบาต](/m/kammawaca-sut-thon-pha-lae-bat-5507/) at Wat Si
  Khom Kham, Phayao — the formal act of the saṅgha bound with a
  **[thon](/a?s=entity:sut_thon) rite for the robe and the bowl**. Beside it,
  catalogued as Vinaya and containing
  [monastic ritual, astrology and chants together](/m/pithikam-song-holasat-lae-bot-suat-mon-4954/),
  sits ms 4954 from [Wat Phra That Lampang Luang](/place/wat-phra-that-lampang-luang/).

## Notes

- **Separate the code from the rite.** The *Pācittiya*/*Samantapāsādika* legal core
  and the *kammavācā*/*parivāsa*/*uppasombot* performance texts are two different
  objects under one label. Pulling them apart is the obvious next pass and would
  move a good share of this sub-genre toward
  [liturgy](/a?s=genre:liturgy_chanting), where the same recitational logic governs
  the paritta and the [thon](/a?s=entity:sut_thon).
- **The ordination liturgy is the part still in use.** The kammavācā is the
  operative script of an act performed at Lanna temples this month.
  [Browse the basket](/browse?subgenre=Vinaya) sorted by date and the tail is all
  ordination and probation texts, copied into the 1960s.
- **Read it beside the customary law.** The Vinaya is the saṅgha's constitution; the
  [customary law](/a?s=genre:law_customary) corpus is the muang's. Both are copied
  by the same hands in the same script, and both reason outward from the incident to
  the rule — a comparison the archive makes possible and nobody has yet run.
- **A transcription priority.** Exactly **one** of the 494 carries page text in the
  archive — ms 5507, above — so everything on this page about the palm-leaf side is
  read off titles, dates and provenance. The highest-value target is the
  probation-and-restoration cluster at Wat Sung Men and Wat Ban Ueam: a documented,
  still-practised judicial procedure that nothing in the digested contributed
  volumes covers.
