## What this is

The **Mahāsamaya Sutta** (มหาสมัยสูตร, "the Great Assembly") is a canonical Pali
discourse, and it is unlike almost anything else in the canon: it is mostly a
**list of names**. The Buddha sits in a forest with five hundred arahants; the
gods of ten world-systems come to look at him; and the sutta records who showed
up. That is the text. In this collection it turns up sixteen times on palm leaf
and mulberry paper, and then — four centuries later and in a different kind of
book altogether — it turns up again, reprinted inside a working manual of
**saiyasat** with the instruction to *memorise the names*. This page is about
why a roll-call became one of the most-copied protective texts in the north, and
what a magician wanted with it.

## The night itself

*Tradition holds —* the scene is set in the Great Wood near Kapilavatthu, the
Buddha's home country, with a company of about five hundred monks, "all of them
arahants." Word gets around the heavens. Gods from ten world-systems come
crowding in to see the Blessed One and his Sangha — and the sutta's own framing
is that the crowd is so large it needs describing.

Four gods of the **Pure Abodes**, the highest heavens, watch the traffic and
decide they should go too — "let each of us recite one verse in his presence."
The manual that reprints the sutta in this archive keeps the simile the Pali
uses for how they travel: they vanish from their heaven and appear in front of
the Buddha as quickly *"as a strong man might stretch out a bent arm, or bend an
outstretched arm."* Then they speak, one after another. The first says that
today is the day of the Great Assembly, that the hosts of gods have gathered,
and that they have come to see the unconquered Sangha. The second describes the
monks in the assembly as composed and upright, *"guarding their faculties as a
charioteer holds the reins."* The third says they have uprooted their defilement
like a nail, a bolt-pin, a dam-post pulled out of the ground, and calls them
*"young [nāgas](/a?s=entity:naga), tamed by the One with the divine eye."* The
fourth promises that whoever has gone to the Buddha for refuge will not fall to
the woeful states.

And then the Buddha turns to the monks and says the thing that makes this text
what it is: *"Monks, we shall now tell the names of the devas. Hold them in
mind."* What follows is the roll-call — rank after rank of gods, named, placed,
and counted.

## What the catalogue holds

**Sixteen witnesses** carry the Mahāsamaya in this collection, and they cluster
where the north's great manuscript libraries are: six at
[Wat Sung Men](/place/wat-sung-men/) in Phrae — more than any other house — with
the rest spread across Nan, Lampang, Phayao and Chiang Mai. The dated ones run
from **1792 to 1894**, a full century of steady recopying. Most are palm leaf;
two are mulberry paper.

The revealing pattern is not the count but the **company it keeps**. Seven of
the sixteen are catalogued as bundles rather than single texts, and the
neighbours repeat: *Āṭānāṭiya*, *Dhammacakka*, *Mahāmaṅgala*, *Karaṇīya*,
*Gilimānanda*, *Rāhulovāda*. One Nan bundle
(*Palitta sut, Kutsala, Mahasamainya, Kilimananta…*, Wat Muang Tuet, 1890) opens
by naming *paritta* outright. A Chiang Mai bundle at
[Wat Chedi Luang](/place/wat-chedi-luang-chiang-mai/) runs to eighty pages and
strings the Mahāsamaya between the Dhammacakka and half a dozen more.

*Inference —* these are not study copies of an interesting discourse. They are
**chanting kits**. The Mahāsamaya travels with the standard protective repertoire
because it *is* one of the protective texts — which is exactly what you would
expect of a sutta whose content is the names of every god who ever came to pay
respect. Recite the list, and the list is present. See
[liturgy & chanting](/a?s=genre:liturgy_chanting) for the wider repertoire and
[Buddhist canonical](/a?s=genre:buddhist_canonical) for the shelf it is
catalogued on, or [browse the canonical genre](/browse?genre=buddhist_canonical)
directly.

## The magician's directory

Here is where the archive earns its keep, because the same text surfaces on the
other side of the collection entirely.

The **[Mind Stream Key to Saiyasat](/m/mind-current-key-saiyasat-6984/)** — a
printed manual of magical practice, not a liturgical book — stops in the middle
of its curriculum and reprints the Mahāsamaya Sutta in full (p.120–123). It says
plainly why. The passage comes immediately after the instruction that the
*phutto* breathing practice must be established "before transmission and
empowerment of the later-part saiyasat text is given," and it introduces the
sutta like a reference work:

*"…so that it may be studied how the devas' own selves appeared to the Buddha,
how they appeared to the disciples, and how an ordinary human being like
ourselves would see a deva — and where a deva resides. Please read this over
several times, and one should also memorise the names of the important devas, as
this is of benefit for saiyasat rites."*

*Inference —* to a chanter the roll-call is a merit-bearing recitation. To a
practitioner who intends to **invite a god into a rite**, the very same roll-call
is an address book: who exists, what rank they hold, where they live, and what
to call them. Nothing in the text changes. Its function changes completely
depending on which shelf you take it down from — and this collection holds both
shelves, which is the whole argument for keeping
[magic & ritual](/a?s=genre:magic_ritual) and the canon in one catalogue. The
same manual's method for questioning a consecrated image — hold it, breathe
*phut* and *tho*, then recite until the deva comes — is described on the
[Khun Phaen page](/a?s=entity:khun_phaen), and it is the practical end of this
same idea.

## Four syllables

The compression goes one step further. In
**[Yantra Inscription Formulas, Khom mixed with Thai](/m/yantra-inscription-formulas-mixed-khom-thai-6977/)**
(p.20) there is a two-column table of *หัวใจ* — "heart" formulas, each a short
syllable-string standing in for a whole text or being. Reading down it: the
Yaksha King, the Lord's Weapon, the Diamond Armour, the Heart of the
[Yantra](/a?s=entity:yantra) itself, the Heart of
[Khun Phaen](/a?s=entity:khun_phaen) — and, near the top,
**กาละกัญชามะหาภิสมา**, glossed *หัวใจมหาสมัย*, the Heart of the Great Assembly.

A sutta of gods without number, reduced to a handful of syllables a practitioner
can inscribe or mutter. *Inference —* the heart-formula is how this tradition
carries a text it cannot carry whole; see [katha](/a?s=entity:katha) for the
mechanism in general. That the Mahāsamaya has one at all is a measure of how
badly it was wanted at working scale.

## Notes

- **The bundles are the finding.** A title search finds sixteen Mahāsamaya
  witnesses, but their value is in the company: any fresh catalogue record that
  lists a bundle's contents rather than its first text makes the north's actual
  chanting repertoire more visible. Seven of these sixteen name the Mahāsamaya
  somewhere other than first, and would be invisible to a search that only read
  a record's opening title.
- **Read alongside [liturgy & chanting](/a?s=genre:liturgy_chanting) and
  [katha](/a?s=entity:katha).** This page follows one text across the seam
  between canon and practice; those two hold the general cases on either side.
- **Not yet transcribed.** No page images of the sixteen witnesses have been read
  into the catalogue, so everything above about their *contents* is inferred from
  titles and from the printed editions. A single transcribed Wat Sung Men copy
  would let the northern recension be compared against the printed one.
